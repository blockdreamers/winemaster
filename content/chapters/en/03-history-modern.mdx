<Section id="dutch-bottles" title="The age of merchants and glass bottles">

In chapter 2 we saw wine take root in Western Europe through monasteries and royal trade. In the 17th century a new player arrives: the **Dutch merchants** who ruled the seas.

The Dutch were drinkers as well as traders. They shipped wine and spirits from western France to northern Europe, and used their land-reclamation skills to help drain the marshes of Bordeaux's **Médoc**. On the gravel left behind, some of the world's most famous Cabernet Sauvignon vineyards would later be planted.

Around the same time, another invention changed wine: **strong glass bottles and corks**. In 17th-century England it became possible to make thick, dark glass bottles, and cork stoppers and corkscrews came into use alongside them. Until then wine was kept in barrels and mostly had to be drunk within a year. Now it could be bottled and **aged for a long time**.

When relations between England and France soured in this period, English merchants turned to Portugal. The habit of adding brandy so the wine would survive long voyages developed into **Port** from the **Douro** valley. In Champagne, too, the skills of handling bubbles in the bottle slowly took hold. Fortified and sparkling wines are covered in detail in chapter 29, "Sparkling, sweet and fortified".

</Section>

<Section id="1855" title="1855: Bordeaux's report card">

In 1855, Emperor Napoleon III wanted to showcase Bordeaux, the pride of France, at the **Paris Exposition Universelle**. To prepare, Bordeaux's wine brokers ranked the châteaux. The result is the **Classification of 1855**.

The yardstick was not a tasting score but the **price** the wines had fetched over many years. In other words, it recorded the market's verdict that the wines that sold for more were the better ones.

| Category | What it covers |
|---|---|
| Red | Médoc châteaux plus Château Haut-Brion in Graves, in five tiers from first to fifth growth |
| Sweet white | The châteaux of Sauternes and Barsac, ranked separately |
| Changes since | Almost none. The most famous is Château Mouton Rothschild's promotion from second to first growth in 1973 |

Remarkably, it has barely changed in more than 170 years, which shows how much it still shapes the price and prestige of Bordeaux. The Left and Right Banks and the finer points of the rankings return in chapter 19, "France I: Bordeaux and the South West".

</Section>

<Section id="phylloxera" title="Phylloxera, the plague of Europe's vineyards">

In the mid-19th century, disaster after disaster struck Europe's vineyards. First came powdery mildew (oidium) in the 1850s, then downy mildew, both fungal diseases from North America. Then came the most fearsome enemy of all.

**Phylloxera** is an aphid-like insect about 1 mm long. Underground, it feeds on the sap of vine **roots**, and as the damaged roots rot, the vine withers and dies within a few years. It arrived in southern France in the **1860s**, hidden on plants imported from North America, and over the following decades spread across France, most of Europe, and on to Australia, South Africa and California. A large share of Europe's vineyards was lost.

The key to the solution lay where the problem came from. North America's native grapes had lived alongside phylloxera and evolved **resistance** in their roots. The answer was **grafting**.

- Below ground, a **rootstock** from an American grape species that resists phylloxera
- Above ground, the trunk and fruit of a **Vitis vinifera** variety that makes delicious wine

The fruit is still Chardonnay or Cabernet Sauvignon, but the roots can withstand phylloxera. Most of the world's vineyards are still grafted this way. In places phylloxera never reached, such as **Chile**, many vines still grow on their own roots.

> The roots of today's French wine are, quite literally, American.

After phylloxera, wine was scarce, and fakes and false claims of origin multiplied. To stop them, France created **AOC (Appellation d'Origine Contrôlée)**, its system of protected place names, in 1935. It became the model for classification systems in other countries. You will learn to read these on a label in chapter 13, "Reading a label".

</Section>

<Section id="new-world" title="Prohibition and the Judgment of Paris">

Wine has a long history outside Europe, too. Spanish missionaries planted vines in South America in the 16th century to make wine for Mass, and vines reached the Cape in South Africa in the 17th century and Australia in the late 18th. Countries outside Europe whose wine industries were built mainly by European settlers are often called the **New World**, while long-established European regions such as France, Italy and Spain are the **Old World**.

The New World had its own trials. In the United States, **Prohibition** banned the production and sale of alcohol **from 1920 to 1933**. There were exceptions, such as sacramental wine and small amounts made at home, but many commercial wineries closed and the industry took decades to recover.

The turning point came in May 1976 at a tasting in Paris. Steven Spurrier, an English wine merchant, set top French wines beside California wines and had French wine experts taste them **blind**, with the labels hidden. The result upended everyone's expectations.

| Category | Winner | Grape |
|---|---|---|
| White | Chateau Montelena 1973 (California) | Chardonnay |
| Red | Stag's Leap Wine Cellars 1973 (California, Napa) | Cabernet Sauvignon |

The event became known as the **Judgment of Paris**, and it shattered the belief that great wine came only from France. It was also the decisive moment that made **Napa Valley** a world-class region.

</Section>

<Section id="today" title="Today's wine map">

Since the 1980s the wine world has grown quickly. Here are a few of the big trends.

- **The rise of the New World**: Shiraz from Australia's **Barossa Valley**, Sauvignon Blanc from New Zealand's **Marlborough**, Argentine Malbec and Chilean Cabernet Sauvignon have all won places on the world market. The New World also popularised putting the **grape name** in big letters on the label instead of the region.
- **Technology spreads**: temperature-controlled stainless steel tanks and hygienic winemaking have greatly reduced faulty wines. **Screwcaps** have become common, led by Australia and New Zealand.
- **New regions**: interest keeps widening, to sparkling wine from southern England, China and Japan, and qvevri wine from Georgia, the birthplace of wine. We meet them again in chapter 28, "Emerging regions and Asia".
- **Climate change**: harvests are getting earlier and alcohol levels higher, and every region is looking for ways to adapt. It is also a theme of chapter 9, "Climate and terroir".

Today the line between Old World and New World is less sharp than it was. New World producers seek out cool sites to make delicate wines, Old World producers embrace new technology, and the two are growing more alike.

</Section>

<Section id="recap" title="Key points">

- In the 17th century **Dutch merchants** led the wine trade and drained the Médoc marshes. **Strong glass bottles and corks** made long ageing in bottle possible.
- The **1855 Classification** was drawn up for the Paris Exposition based on **prices** at the time, with **five tiers** for reds. Apart from Mouton Rothschild's 1973 promotion, it is almost unchanged.
- **Phylloxera**, a root-feeding insect from North America, ravaged Europe's vineyards from the **1860s**. The fix: **grafting onto American rootstocks**.
- In the chaos after phylloxera, France created the **AOC** system in 1935.
- After the gap left by US **Prohibition (1920–1933)**, California wines won the **1976 Judgment of Paris**, opening the age of the New World.
