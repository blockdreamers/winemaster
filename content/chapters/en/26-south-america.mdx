<Section id="andes" title="Two countries divided by the Andes">

The two leading players in South American wine are **Chile** and **Argentina**. In both, wine history began in the 16th century when Spanish missionaries planted grapes for sacramental wine, and they took their modern shape after French varieties arrived in the 19th century.

What separates them is the **Andes** mountain range, but the mountains give each country a different gift. To Chile they give **snowmelt** and cold night air; to Argentina, **altitude** and dry sunshine. Chile has one more thing as well: a **cold ocean**.

| Region | Country | Main varieties | Style |
|---|---|---|---|
| Casablanca, Leyda | Chile | Sauvignon Blanc, Chardonnay, Pinot Noir | Zesty, high-acid whites thanks to sea breezes |
| Maipo Valley | Chile | Cabernet Sauvignon | Balanced reds with blackcurrant and mint |
| Colchagua | Chile | Carmenère, Cabernet, Syrah | Rich, ripe, full-bodied reds |
| Mendoza (Luján de Cuyo) | Argentina | Malbec, Cabernet Sauvignon | Smooth, dense plum and blackberry |
| Uco Valley | Argentina | Malbec, Chardonnay | High-altitude acidity and floral notes |
| Salta (Cafayate) | Argentina | Torrontés, Malbec | Aromatic whites, dense high-altitude reds |

</Section>

<Section id="chile-shape" title="Chile: a long, thin country">

Chile stretches more than 4,000 km from north to south, yet averages less than 200 km from east to west. To the north is the Atacama, the driest desert on Earth; to the east the Andes; to the west the Pacific; and to the south glacier country. Walled in by natural barriers on every side, it kept out the **phylloxera** we saw in Chapter 3, "History of Wine II". That's why many vines are still grown ungrafted.

Slice Chile from west to east and the character of its wine becomes clear at a glance.

- **Humboldt Current**: cold water flowing up from Antarctica sends fog and chilly winds to the coast. It is a textbook case of the ocean cooling effect from Chapter 9, "Climate and Terroir".
- **Coastal range**: a wall that blocks some of the sea wind. Where the range is low or broken, breezes reach far inland.
- **Central Valley**: warm, dry flatland between the coastal range and the Andes. Its Mediterranean climate has almost no summer rain, so grapes ripen well.
- **Andes**: at night cold air flows down from the mountains, widening the day-night temperature swing, and snowmelt provides irrigation water.

That's why Chile now lets labels add **Costa** (coast), **Entre Cordilleras** (between the ranges) or **Andes** to the north-south region name. Even within one valley, where it sits from west to east matters.

</Section>

<Section id="chile-regions" title="Chile’s main regions and Carmenère">

**Maipo Valley**, surrounding the capital Santiago, is the historic heart of Chilean wine. **Alto Maipo** in the Andean foothills has cool nights and free-draining gravel soils, and produces Chile's most celebrated **Cabernet Sauvignon**. Ripe blackcurrant with a refreshing lift of mint or eucalyptus is its hallmark.

**Casablanca Valley** lies between Santiago and the port city of Valparaíso. Close to the sea, it gets strong morning fog and afternoon breezes. A cool region pioneered from the 1980s onward, it is good for zesty **Sauvignon Blanc**, Chardonnay and Pinot Noir. Its southern neighbours **San Antonio** and **Leyda** are even closer to the ocean and racier still. For the Sauvignon Blanc story, see Chapter 14, "International White Varieties I".

**Colchagua Valley**, part of the Rapel Valley, is warmer and produces rich, generous reds. **Carmenère**, Cabernet Sauvignon, Syrah and blends of them are its strengths. Further south, **Maule** still has old, dry-farmed Carignan and País vineyards that are drawing fresh attention.

Now for Chile's emblem, **Carmenère**. It was originally a Bordeaux variety. It came to Chile with other Bordeaux grapes in the 19th century, but in Bordeaux itself it nearly disappeared after phylloxera because it ripened late and yielded unreliably. In Chile it was long **mixed in with Merlot and called Merlot**. Then in 1994 a French ampelographer discovered that many of Chile's "Merlot" vineyards were actually Carmenère.

> A Bordeaux grape thought to be lost had been living on the other side of the world for over a century, wearing a different name tag.

Carmenère ripens much later than Merlot. When fully ripe it shows blackberry and plum with notes of pepper and coffee, and soft tannins. When underripe, **green, bell-pepper-like aromas** take over. Chile's long, dry autumns let it ripen all the way.

</Section>

<Section id="argentina" title="Argentina: climbing high to find cool">

Argentina's vineyards lie on the eastern foothills of the Andes, far from the sea, in a **dry continental climate**. With little rain, pests and diseases are rare, but the sun is intense and the days are hot. So Argentina finds its cool through **altitude**. The higher you go, the lower the temperature, and the nights especially get cold, widening the **day-night temperature swing**. Grapes ripen fully by day and hold on to acidity and aroma at night.

The vineyards are watered by Andean snowmelt, with a long tradition of channelling it into the desert through canals. On the other hand, summer **hail** and the hot, dry wind called the **Zonda** are constant worries.

**Mendoza** is the central region, holding the largest share of Argentina's vineyards. They sit at roughly 800–1,500 m above sea level.

- **Luján de Cuyo** and **Maipú**: traditional areas south of Mendoza city with many old Malbec vineyards, producing a smooth, rich style.
- **Uco Valley**: further south-west, high ground right beneath the Andes. It is drawing attention for Malbec and Chardonnay with vivid acidity and clear violet aromas.

**Malbec** originally comes from **Cahors** in South-West France. It arrived in Argentina in the mid-19th century and won far greater fame there than at home. Argentine Malbec is deep purple, with plum, blackberry, violet and soft tannins. Drinking it with *asado*, beef grilled over charcoal, is one of the country's defining images. Revisit Malbec in Chapter 18, "Regional Signature Varieties".

In the north, the **Cafayate** area of **Salta** has vineyards well above 2,000 m, among the highest in the world. Its signature grape is the white **Torrontés**. It smells showy, like rose, peach and Muscat, but usually tastes dry, so it's easy to mistake it for a sweet wine from the nose alone. In the south, cool **Patagonia** grows Pinot Noir.

</Section>

<Section id="recap" title="Key points">

- Chile and Argentina are neighbours across the **Andes**. In Chile the **ocean** cools the grapes; in Argentina, **altitude** does.
- Chile is long and thin. The **Humboldt Current**, coastal range, Central Valley and Andes run side by side from west to east, and natural barriers keep it **free of phylloxera**.
- **Maipo** means Cabernet Sauvignon, **Casablanca** means sea-breeze Sauvignon Blanc and Chardonnay, and **Colchagua** means rich reds.
- **Carmenère** disappeared from Bordeaux and was wrongly called Merlot in Chile until its identity was revealed in 1994.
- Argentina's heart, **Mendoza**, is high ground at 800–1,500 m. **Malbec**, originally from Cahors, flourished here, and the **Uco Valley** is higher and fresher.
- **Torrontés** from **Salta** is an aromatic dry white.
