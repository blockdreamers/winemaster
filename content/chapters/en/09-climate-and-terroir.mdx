<Section id="climate-weather" title="Climate is not weather">

First, two words to separate. **Climate** is a region's weather averaged over decades, while **weather** is what actually happens this year, today. "Bordeaux has a maritime climate" is about climate; "Bordeaux had an unusually hot summer this year" is about weather.

In wine, climate decides **which grapes can be planted and what styles result**. Weather creates **the differences from year to year**, the personality of each vintage. The frost, hail and harvest rain from chapter 8, "The vine's year", all belong to weather.

Vines cannot survive the winter if it is too cold, and in too much heat they never rest and lose their acid. So most of the world's major wine regions lie in a band between **30 and 50 degrees of latitude**, north and south. Open a map and you will find France, Italy, Spain and the western US in the north, and Chile, Argentina, South Africa, Australia and New Zealand in the south, all inside this band.

</Section>

<Section id="cool-warm" title="Cool climates, warm climates">

Regions are broadly split into three groups by their average growing-season temperature. The cut-off values vary slightly between textbooks, but the pattern is the same.

| | Cool climate | Moderate climate | Warm climate |
|---|---|---|---|
| Average growing-season temperature | Below about 16.5°C | About 16.5–18.5°C | About 18.5–21°C |
| Acidity | High | Medium to high | On the low side |
| Alcohol | On the low side | Medium | High |
| Body | Light | Medium | Full |
| Fruit aromas | Green apple, lemon, cranberry, red cherry | Stone fruit, red and black fruit | Tropical fruit, blackberry, jammy fruit |
| Tannin in reds | Can be light or green | Ripe | Soft and plush |
| Examples | Champagne, Mosel, Chablis | Bordeaux, northern Rhône | Barossa Valley, southern Italy |

The same grape changes its face with the climate. **Chardonnay** becomes a sharp wine of lemon, green apple and wet stone in cool Chablis, and a lush wine of peach and pineapple in warm regions. **Syrah**, too, shows peppery notes in the cool northern Rhône and leans towards rich blackberry and chocolate in hot Barossa.

> If you can take a sip and guess "this came from somewhere cool", you have started reading the wine map.

As we saw in chapter 1, "What is wine?", sugar is the raw material of alcohol. The warmer it is, the more sugar builds up, so alcohol rises; and acid is used up faster in the heat, so it falls. Those two facts explain almost everything in the table.

</Section>

<Section id="climate-types" title="Continental, maritime, Mediterranean">

Apart from how warm or cool they are, climates are also classed by how temperature and rainfall move through the year.

| Climate type | Features | Effect on wine | Classic regions |
|---|---|---|---|
| Continental | Hot, short summers and cold winters. Big annual range | Risk of spring frost and autumn cold. Big differences between years | Burgundy, Alsace, Mendoza |
| Maritime | The sea keeps the annual range small and rain falls evenly all year | Long ripening period. Humidity means watching for rot | Bordeaux |
| Mediterranean | Hot dry summers, mild wet winters | Grapes ripen reliably. Little difference between years | Southern Rhône, California, South Australia |

Think of how coastal cities are milder in winter and cooler in summer, while inland cities swelter in summer and freeze in winter. Large bodies of water are slow to heat and slow to cool, so they soften the temperature around them. Bordeaux blends several grapes precisely to hedge against its changeable maritime weather.

</Section>

<Section id="local-factors" title="How landscape shapes climate">

Even at the same latitude and in the same climate type, conditions vary greatly from vineyard to vineyard. These are the factors that change a site's temperature and sunlight.

- **Latitude** – the closer to the equator, the hotter; the closer to the poles, the cooler. But high-latitude regions have long summer days, so they get many hours of sun.
- **Altitude** – temperature drops as you climb, by roughly 0.6°C for every 100 m. Argentina's **Mendoza** lies at a hot latitude, but its vineyards sit high in the Andean foothills and keep their acidity.
- **Slope and aspect** – slopes catch sunlight head-on, drain well, and let cold air flow downhill so frost does less damage. **South-facing** slopes get more sun in the Northern Hemisphere, **north-facing** ones in the Southern. The steep south-facing slopes of Germany's **Mosel** are the classic example.
- **Water (sea, rivers, lakes)** – large bodies of water moderate temperature swings, and rivers can reflect sunlight onto the vineyards and warm them.
- **Fog and ocean currents** – where cold currents run offshore, fog and cool breezes push inland. Coastal parts of California's **Sonoma** and coastal regions of Chile and South Africa are cool thanks to this.
- **Diurnal range** – hot days and cool nights let sugar build by day while acid is preserved at night. It is the secret of wines that are ripe yet fresh.
- **Wind** – it dries off humidity and reduces rot, but if too strong it damages the vines. The cold, dry northerly **mistral** of the Rhône valley is famous.

</Section>

<Section id="soil-terroir" title="Soil and terroir">

After climate, the next most important factor is **soil**. The way soil affects wine is more physical than you might think. Rather than mineral flavours passing straight into the wine, the key is **how it handles water, heat and nutrients**.

| Soil | Properties | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Gravel | Drains well, soaks up the day's heat and releases it at night | Cabernet Sauvignon in Bordeaux's Médoc |
| Clay | Holds water and stays cool | Merlot in Pomerol on Bordeaux's Right Bank |
| Limestone, chalk | Drains well yet stores a moderate amount of water | Champagne, Burgundy |
| Slate | Holds heat and drains well | Riesling in the Mosel |

Interestingly, vines do not like soil that is too fertile. With plenty of nutrients and water, they grow lush leaves and shoots and put less energy into the fruit. Vines that root deep in poor soil often give small but concentrated fruit.

The word that brings all this together is **terroir**: the unique environment of a vineyard, combining its soil, landscape, climate and the small climate within it. Many people also include the growing and winemaking traditions handed down on that land. In Burgundy, wines from plots separated by a single path or wall are judged differently, and these small parcels defined by terroir are called **climats**. More on this in chapter 20, "France II: Burgundy and Beaujolais".

We already know that the same chilli pepper gives powder of a different colour and heat depending on whose sun and soil it grew in, and that the same tea plant tastes different from one hillside to the next. Terroir applies that same intuition to wine.

</Section>

<Section id="recap" title="Key points">

- **Climate** is the long-term pattern, **weather** the conditions each year. Differences in weather create **vintages**.
- Most major regions lie between **30 and 50 degrees of latitude**.
- **Cool climates** give high-acid, lower-alcohol, lighter wines; **warm climates** give lower-acid, higher-alcohol, richer wines.
- **Continental** climates have a big annual range, **maritime** ones are mild and damp, **Mediterranean** ones have hot, dry summers.
- **Altitude, slope and aspect, water, fog and currents, diurnal range and wind** change a site's climate.
- Soil shapes wine through **water, heat and nutrients**. All of this environment together is **terroir**.
