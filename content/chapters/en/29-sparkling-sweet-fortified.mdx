<Section id="three-families" title="One more twist to the fermentation formula">

In Chapter 1, "What Is Wine?", we summed up fermentation as **sugar → alcohol + carbon dioxide**. The three families of wine in this chapter are all made by deliberately twisting some part of that formula.

| Family | What changes in the formula | Signature wines |
|---|---|---|
| Sparkling | **Trap the carbon dioxide** that would escape | Champagne, Cava, Prosecco, Asti |
| Sweet | **Concentrate the grapes' sugar**, or stop fermentation early | Sauternes, Tokaji, icewine, Recioto |
| Fortified | **Add a spirit** during or after fermentation | Sherry, Port, Madeira |

Sparkling wines ultimately divide by **where the second fermentation happens**. In the bottle you'll sell, it's the traditional method; in a big tank, it's the tank method. Just as a bottle of makgeolli (Korean rice wine) left to sit makes its cap bulge, sealing a fermenting wine dissolves the carbon dioxide into it. Differences in bubble size and aroma start from this difference in place.

</Section>

<Section id="traditional-method" title="The traditional method: once more in the bottle">

The **traditional method** (méthode traditionnelle) used for Champagne, Cava, Crémant and Franciacorta is the most labour-intensive. Let's follow it step by step.

1. **Make the base wine.** First, a light, high-acid dry wine is made. That's why cool regions have the advantage.
2. **Blending (assemblage).** Wines from different plots, different varieties and sometimes different years are blended to match the house style. Non-vintage (NV) wines that taste similar every year are made this way.
3. **Tirage.** Sugar and yeast are mixed into the base wine, which is bottled and sealed with a crown cap.
4. **Second fermentation in bottle.** The yeast eats the sugar, raising the alcohol by just over 1%, and carbon dioxide dissolves into the wine, building pressure in the bottle to roughly 5–6 atmospheres.
5. **Ageing on lees.** Once fermentation is done, the yeast dies and becomes sediment (lees), slowly breaking down (**autolysis**) and giving aromas of bread, brioche and biscuit. Even non-vintage Champagne must age at least 15 months before release, at least 12 of them on the lees, and vintage Champagne at least 3 years.
6. **Remuage (riddling).** With the bottles upside down, they are turned and tilted little by little to gather the sediment in the neck. This used to be done by hand on wooden racks, but today it's mostly done by machines called **gyropalettes**.
7. **Dégorgement (disgorging).** Only the neck is dipped in a freezing liquid to freeze the sediment, then the cap is removed and the pressure pushes out the plug of ice.
8. **Dosage.** The lost volume is topped up with **liqueur d'expédition**, a mix of wine and sugar. The amount of sugar sets the final sweetness. Finally the bottle is sealed with a cork and wire cage.

> Champagne's bready aroma comes not from the grapes but from dead yeast. The longer the lees ageing, the deeper that aroma.

The sweetness set by dosage appears on the label like this. The ranges are residual sugar per litre.

| Term | Residual sugar (g/L) | Impression |
|---|---|---|
| Brut Nature | 0–3, no sugar added | Driest |
| Extra Brut | 0–6 | Very dry |
| Brut | Under 12 | Dry. The most common term |
| Extra Dry | 12–17 | Just slightly sweet |
| Sec | 17–32 | Somewhat sweet |
| Demi-Sec | 32–50 | Sweet |
| Doux | Over 50 | Very sweet |

The confusing part is that **Extra Dry is sweeter than Brut**. It trips people up in exams and in wine shops alike. We first met lees ageing and autolysis in Chapter 12, "Oak and Ageing", and the story of the Champagne region continues in the wiki's Champagne entry.

</Section>

<Section id="tank-and-asti" title="The tank method, the Asti method and the transfer method">

Not every sparkling wine wants bready aromas. If the grape's own fresh aromas are the star, it's better not to leave the wine long on its lees.

The **tank method (Charmat or Martinotti)** carries out the second fermentation in a large **pressurised stainless steel tank**. Once fermentation is finished, the wine is filtered under pressure and bottled. It is fast and economical, and above all it keeps **primary aromas** like pear, apple and white flowers **vivid**. Its signature is **Prosecco** from Italy's Veneto, made mainly from **Glera**. Prosecco also uses terms like Brut, Extra Dry and Dry, and here too Extra Dry is sweeter than Brut.

The **Asti method** doesn't ferment twice. Moscato (Moscato Bianco) juice is kept chilled until needed, then fermented **only once** in a pressurised tank. At first the carbon dioxide is released, then at a certain point the tank is closed to trap it. When the desired alcohol is reached, **fermentation is stopped by chilling and filtration**, leaving sugar behind. So **Asti** becomes a sweet sparkling wine, low in alcohol at around 7–9% and full of grapey aroma. **Moscato d'Asti**, with gentler bubbles and alcohol around 5%, is made on the same principle.

The **transfer method** sits between the two. The second fermentation and lees ageing happen in bottle, but instead of riddling, the bottles are emptied into a pressurised tank, filtered all at once and rebottled. It is sometimes used for sizes where riddling is awkward, such as half bottles or very large bottles.

| Method | Where the second fermentation happens | Aromatic focus | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| Traditional | The bottle it is sold in | Autolysis (bread, biscuit) plus fruit | Champagne, Cava, Crémant |
| Transfer | Bottle, then moved to a tank and filtered | Similar to traditional | Some New World sparkling |
| Tank | Pressurised tank | Fresh fruit, flowers | Prosecco |
| Asti | Pressurised tank (one fermentation) | Grapey aroma, sweetness | Asti, Moscato d'Asti |

</Section>

<Section id="sweet-wines" title="Sweet wines: how to keep the sugar">

A dry wine is one where the yeast has eaten all the sugar. To make a sweet wine, you either **make the sugar so concentrated** the yeast can't finish it, or **stop fermentation** before it does. The sweetness levels from Chapter 4, "Wine Styles at a Glance", turn into real techniques here. There are four main ways to concentrate the grapes' sugar.

**1. Late harvest.** Harvest is delayed so the grapes ripen further on the vine, and some are left to lose water and shrivel. Alsace's **Vendanges Tardives** and Germany's **Spätlese** and **Auslese** belong to this group.

**2. Noble rot.** When a fungus called **Botrytis cinerea** makes tiny holes in the skins of ripe grapes, water evaporates and sugar and acid become concentrated. It also creates distinctive aromas like honey, apricot jam, ginger and saffron. The site needs mist rising from a river or lake in the morning and clear, dry afternoons, because with constant rain the same fungus becomes grey rot, which ruins wine. Each bunch is infected at a different pace, so pickers must select by hand over several passes, which is why these wines are inevitably expensive.
- **Sauternes**: south of Bordeaux, where mist forms as the Ciron meets the Garonne. Semillon leads, blended with Sauvignon Blanc. New oak ageing is common (Chapter 19).
- **Tokaji Aszú**: Hungary. The main variety is **Furmint**. Noble-rotted berries (aszú) are collected separately, made into a paste, and soaked in base wine or fermenting must to extract sugar and aroma. Sweetness has traditionally been expressed in **puttonyos** (Chapter 24).
- Germany's **Beerenauslese (BA)** and **Trockenbeerenauslese (TBA)** are also top-tier sweet wines made from noble-rotted grapes.

**3. Frozen grapes (icewine/Eiswein).** Grapes are left on the vine into midwinter, then picked frozen and pressed on a night below -7 to -8°C. The water stays behind as ice, and only a little juice, richly concentrated in sugar and acid, runs out. Unlike noble rot, there is no fungal character, so the hallmarks are **clear, pure fruit and thrilling acidity**. Germany and Austria's **Eiswein** and Ontario's **Icewine** in Canada are famous; Canada uses a lot of Riesling and Vidal (Chapter 25).

**4. Dried grapes.** Harvested grapes are dried for weeks or months on straw mats or racks in well-ventilated rooms. Like making dried persimmons, the water leaves and the sugar concentrates. In Italy such wines are called **passito**, and the drying process **appassimento**. Veneto's **Recioto della Valpolicella** is a sweet red made from dried Corvina grapes; ferment the same grapes all the way to dryness and you get **Amarone** (Chapter 22). Tuscany's **Vin Santo** is also made from dried grapes.

| Method | Principle | Aroma clues | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| Late harvest | Extra ripening on the vine | Ripe fruit, tropical fruit | Vendanges Tardives, Spätlese |
| Noble rot | Fungus evaporates the water | Honey, apricot jam, ginger, saffron | Sauternes, Tokaji Aszú, TBA |
| Frozen grapes | Ice holds back the water | Pure fruit, high acidity | Eiswein, Canadian icewine |
| Dried grapes | Drying after harvest | Raisin, dried fig | Recioto, Vin Santo |

Whatever the method, the requirement for a good sweet wine is the same: **acidity to hold up the sweetness**. A sweet wine short on acid is sticky like syrup and quickly cloying. That's why high-acid varieties like Riesling, Chenin Blanc and Furmint shine in sweet wines.

</Section>

<Section id="sherry" title="Sherry: flor and solera">

From here on we're in **fortified wine**: wine with grape spirit added to raise its alcohol. **When** the spirit goes in decides the sweetness. Added after fermentation, the wine is dry; added during it, the wine is sweet.

**Sherry** is made around **Jerez** in Andalusia, southern Spain. The main variety is **Palomino**, a grape with modest aromas of its own. It grows in white chalky soil called **albariza**, and the spirit is added to a **fully fermented dry wine**. Sherry's character is decided by how it ages next.

**Biological ageing.** If wine fortified to around 15% is left in casks that aren't completely full, a white film of yeast called **flor** grows on the surface. Flor protects the wine from oxygen, keeping it pale, and leaves aromas of freshly baked bread, almonds and a salty sea tang. Sherry made this way is **Fino**, and the lighter, saltier version aged in the seaside town of Sanlúcar de Barrameda is **Manzanilla**.

**Oxidative ageing.** Raise the alcohol to 17% or more and flor can't survive. The wine slowly meets air, turns brown and gains aromas of walnut, toffee and leather. This is **Oloroso**. It is heavier and rounder than Fino and dry by default.

**Go through both** and you get **Amontillado**. It starts as a Fino, and once the flor dies off (or is deliberately killed by adding spirit), oxidative ageing continues. It combines Fino's saltiness with Oloroso's nuttiness.

For sweetness, separately made sweet wine is blended in, or the wine is made from the start from sun-dried **Pedro Ximénez (PX)** grapes. PX is a very sweet Sherry, nearly black in colour, tasting of raisins and fig syrup.

The system used to age Sherry is the **solera**. Casks are arranged in several tiers, and wine for bottling is drawn **only in part from the oldest tier (the solera)**. What's removed is immediately replaced with wine from the tier above (the **criadera**), which is in turn topped up from the tier above it, and new wine goes into the top tier.

Think of Korean *ssi-ganjang*, a "mother" soy sauce kept going for generations. Take out some old soy sauce and pour in new to keep it going, and the taste in the jar stays almost the same year after year. The solera works the same way, producing **a consistent style every year**, which is why most Sherry has no vintage.

</Section>

<Section id="port" title="Port: sweetness from stopped fermentation">

**Port** is made from grapes grown in the **Douro** Valley in northern Portugal. On terraced vineyards cut into steep schist slopes, native varieties such as **Touriga Nacional**, Touriga Franca and Tinta Roriz (Tempranillo) are blended.

The crucial difference from Sherry is that **the spirit is added during fermentation**. Colour and tannin are extracted from the skins quickly and intensely, and while plenty of sugar remains, grape spirit of around 77% alcohol (**aguardente**) is poured in. With the alcohol suddenly high, the yeast stops working. So Port becomes a wine that is **sweet, around 20% alcohol, and deep in colour and tannin**.

Port styles split in two depending on **how they age**.

| Family | Ageing | Main styles | Taste |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ruby family | In large vats or tanks with little oxygen, or in bottle | Ruby, Reserve, LBV, Vintage | Deep black fruit, vivid red colour |
| Tawny family | Long and slow oxidation in small wooden casks | Tawny, 10/20/30/40-year-old Tawny | Nuts, caramel, dried fruit, amber-brown |

- **Ruby**: the basic style, aged briefly to keep its young fruit. **Reserve** is a step up in quality and concentration.
- **LBV (Late Bottled Vintage)**: made from a single year and bottled after 4–6 years in wood. It is designed to be drinkable sooner than Vintage Port.
- **Vintage Port**: made only in outstanding years when the producer "declares" a vintage. It spends about 2 years in wood and is bottled early, then matures **for decades in bottle**. It is barely filtered and throws a sediment, so it needs **decanting**.
- **Tawny**: meeting oxygen in small casks, its colour fades to amber-brown and it takes on nutty aromas. Numbers like **10, 20, 30 or 40 years** are not exact ages but the **average style** of that age. Tawny from a single year is called **Colheita**.

> Ruby matures slowly in bottle; Tawny matures in cask, meeting the air. Even within Port, where it ages decides the taste.

</Section>

<Section id="madeira" title="Madeira: a wine finished by heat and oxygen">

**Madeira** is the name of a Portuguese island in the middle of the Atlantic and of the fortified wine made there. The story goes that the style began in the Age of Exploration, when wine carried on ships across the equator, sloshing around in hot holds, actually tasted better. Today the island recreates that process.

- **Estufagem**: wine in tanks is heated by pipes carrying hot water and aged for several months. It is fast and economical, used for basic Madeira.
- **Canteiro**: casks are kept on the upper floors of warm lodges and aged slowly by natural heat for years or decades. It is more delicate and complex, so fine Madeira uses this method.

Deliberately put through **heat and oxidation** this way, Madeira develops aromas of caramel, roasted nuts, coffee and dried fruit along with **very high acidity**. Because it has already been fully oxidised, it **keeps for months after opening**, and old bottles are astonishingly lively. Fine Madeira is often named after its variety, in order of sweetness.

| Variety | Sweetness |
|---|---|
| Sercial | Driest |
| Verdelho | Medium dry |
| Bual (Boal) | Medium sweet |
| Malmsey (Malvasia) | Sweetest |

The island's most planted variety is the red **Tinta Negra**, used across Madeiras of every sweetness level.

Here are the three fortified wines side by side.

| | Sherry | Port | Madeira |
|---|---|---|---|
| Region | Jerez, Spain | Douro, Portugal | Madeira island, Portugal |
| When spirit is added | After fermentation (dry by default) | During fermentation (sweet) | Depends on the style |
| Key to ageing | Flor and solera | Bottle ageing (Ruby) or cask oxidation (Tawny) | Heat and oxidation |
| Signature aromas | Almond, bread, walnut | Black fruit, or nuts and caramel | Caramel, coffee, roasted nuts |

</Section>

<Section id="recap" title="Key points">

- The three families twist the fermentation formula. Sparkling wine **traps carbon dioxide**, sweet wine **keeps sugar**, and fortified wine **adds spirit**.
- **Traditional method**: tirage → second fermentation in bottle → lees ageing (autolysis) → remuage → dégorgement → dosage. Sweetness runs from Brut Nature to Doux, and **Extra Dry is sweeter than Brut**.
- The **tank method** keeps fresh fruit aromas (Prosecco); the **Asti method** stops a single fermentation, leaving the wine sweet and low in alcohol.
- Sweet wines concentrate sugar through **late harvest, noble rot (Sauternes, Tokaji), frozen grapes (icewine) and dried grapes (passito, Recioto)**. **Acidity** is essential to a good sweet wine.
- **Sherry** is fortified after fermentation. **Biological ageing** under flor (Fino, Manzanilla), **oxidative ageing** in contact with air (Oloroso), or both (Amontillado). The **solera** keeps the style consistent.
- **Port** is fortified during fermentation, so it's sweet. The **Ruby family** (Ruby, LBV, Vintage) is about fruit; the **Tawny family** about nuts and caramel.
- **Madeira** goes through heat and oxidation via **estufagem** or **canteiro**, so it lasts long after opening.
