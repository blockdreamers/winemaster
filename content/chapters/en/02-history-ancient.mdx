<Section id="georgia" title="Clay jars from 8,000 years ago">

Wine was less **invented** than **discovered**. As we saw in chapter 1, crushed grapes start fermenting all by themselves thanks to the yeasts on their skins. The moment people began gathering grapes in containers, wine more or less made itself.

The oldest traces of that moment are found south of the Caucasus mountains, in today's **Georgia**. Analysis of pottery shards from Neolithic villages in southern Georgia revealed wine residues from around **6000 BC**, some eight thousand years ago. Very early signs of winemaking have also turned up in Iran's Zagros mountains and in an Armenian cave, so this whole area is considered the cradle of wine.

Georgia still keeps a tradition that reaches back to those days. Crushed grapes, skins and all, are fermented and aged in a **qvevri**, a large egg-shaped clay jar buried in the ground. The earth keeps the temperature steady, the same reason people around the world have buried food jars to store them. Qvevri winemaking is listed by UNESCO as Intangible Cultural Heritage.

</Section>

<Section id="egypt-phoenicia" title="The pharaohs' drink, the Phoenicians' ships">

Wine soon spread to Mesopotamia and Egypt. These lands were not ideal for vines, so wine was the drink of kings, nobles and temples, while ordinary people mostly drank beer. Egyptian tomb paintings show grapes being picked and trodden underfoot, and the wine jars in Tutankhamun's tomb were marked with the vineyard, the year and the winemaker. They were, in effect, **ancient labels**.

The people who carried wine far across the sea were the **Phoenicians**. From their base on the coast of today's Lebanon, these maritime traders founded outposts all around the Mediterranean and brought vine-growing know-how along with the wine. Carthage in North Africa and southern Spain lay along their routes.

Wine was stored and shipped in the **amphora**, a two-handled clay jar with a pointed base that could be pushed into the sand in a ship's hold or set in a rack. Piles of amphorae are still found in shipwrecks on the Mediterranean seabed.

</Section>

<Section id="greece-rome" title="Greek banquets, Roman roads">

In ancient Greece, wine sat at the heart of culture. The Greeks worshipped **Dionysus**, god of wine and festivity, and called their drinking parties, where men gathered to share poetry and philosophy, the **symposion**, the origin of our word "symposium". Interestingly, the Greeks drank their wine **mixed with water**. They blended the two in a large bowl called a krater, and drinking wine neat was seen as a barbarian habit.

As the Greeks founded colonies in southern Italy, Sicily and places like today's Marseille, they planted vines. Southern Italy had so many vineyards that the Greeks called it a "land of vines".

**Rome** took up the baton. The Romans turned wine into an everyday drink for everyone, and vineyards followed the legions and settlers wherever they went. Many of today's great regions, including the **Rhône** valley, **Bordeaux** (then called Burdigala) and the banks of Germany's **Mosel**, took shape as vineyards in Roman times. The Roman scholar Pliny the Elder described grape varieties and growing methods in detail in his encyclopedia, the *Natural History*.

| Era | Main players | Legacy |
|---|---|---|
| c. 6000 BC | Peoples of the Caucasus (around Georgia) | Oldest winemaking evidence, the qvevri tradition |
| c. 3000 BC on | Egypt | Tomb paintings, jars marked with origin and year |
| 1000s BC on | Phoenicians | Vines spread along Mediterranean trade routes |
| 800s BC on | Greece | The symposion, colonies in southern Italy and southern France |
| 200s BC to AD 400s | Rome | Vineyards across Western Europe, the spread of the barrel |

Rome also saw a quiet revolution. The **wooden barrel** used by the people of Gaul (today's France) gradually replaced the heavy, fragile amphora. That barrel became the starting point of oak ageing, a story that continues in chapter 12, "Oak and ageing".

</Section>

<Section id="monasteries" title="Vineyards kept by monks">

When the Western Roman Empire fell in the 5th century, Europe was thrown into turmoil. The ones who kept the vineyards going were the **Church**. Wine was essential for Mass, and monasteries, with their literate monks, land and labour, were the best-placed institutions to carry on farming vines.

Two orders stand out.

- **The Benedictines** grew powerful around the abbey of **Cluny**, founded in Burgundy in 910, and owned many vineyards given to them as donations.
- **The Cistercians** began in 1098 at **Cîteaux** in Burgundy and valued labour and austerity. **Clos de Vougeot**, the walled vineyard Cistercian monks built up, is still a Burgundy grand cru today.

Working the same plots for centuries and keeping records, the monks noticed that wine from the field right next door tasted different, year after year. They began separating the slopes that caught the most sun and the soils that gave finer wine, plot by plot. This is the root of the idea of **terroir** and of Burgundy's finely divided vineyards, its **climats**, which were inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage site in 2015.

> Burgundy labels name individual vineyards because monks spent nearly a thousand years recording how each plot tasted different.

The **Pinot Noir** and **Chardonnay** that grew in this soil became the face of Burgundy as we know it. The full story waits in chapter 20, "France II: Burgundy and Beaujolais".

</Section>

<Section id="claret" title="Bordeaux, England and the birth of claret">

Another great thread in medieval wine history is **trade**. In 1152, **Eleanor of Aquitaine**, heiress to the Aquitaine region of south-west France, married Henry, Count of Anjou, who two years later became **Henry II** of England. As a result, Aquitaine, Bordeaux included, became a possession of the English crown.

From then on, wine shipments from the port of Bordeaux to England grew enormously. The English called the pale Bordeaux reds of the day **claret**, from the French clairet, and the name is still used in Britain for red Bordeaux.

The arrangement lasted about 300 years, until the **Hundred Years' War** ended in 1453 and Bordeaux returned to France. Yet the bond between Bordeaux and the British market endured, and it is why Bordeaux grew up from the start as an "export wine". The story continues in chapter 3, "Wine history II: phylloxera and the New World", with Dutch merchants and the 1855 Classification.

<Section id="recap" title="Key points">

- The oldest winemaking evidence comes from **around Georgia, c. 6000 BC**. The tradition of the buried clay **qvevri** lives on.
- In Egypt wine was the drink of kings and temples, and the **Phoenicians** spread vines along Mediterranean trade routes.
- **Greece** made wine part of culture (the symposion, drinking it with water), and **Rome** planted vineyards across Western Europe, in the Rhône, Bordeaux and Mosel. Transport shifted **from amphorae to barrels**.
- After Rome, **monasteries** kept the vineyards. Plot-by-plot observation by the **Cistercians**, founded in 1098, is the root of **terroir** and Burgundy's **climats**.
- A royal marriage in 1152 launched the **Bordeaux–England** trade, and the English called red Bordeaux **claret**.
