<Section id="overview" title="One grape, thousands of vineyards">

Burgundy (Bourgogne) is a region in eastern France, running in patches from Chablis in the north to Beaujolais near Lyon in the south. Where Bordeaux blends several grapes, Burgundy almost always uses **a single grape**: **Pinot Noir** for reds and **Chardonnay** for whites. It is the home of the two grapes we met in chapters 14 and 17.

With only one grape, where do the differences in taste come from? From the **vineyard**. Medieval monks spent centuries recording the taste of each plot and drawing boundaries, and the parcels they defined are called **climats**. It is the place where **terroir**, the combination of soil, slope, aspect and microclimate we learned about in chapter 9, "Climate and terroir", shows most clearly.

> Just as every family's homemade soy sauce tastes different even with the same beans and the same starter, the same Pinot Noir tastes different a few dozen metres up or down the hill.

The climate is **continental**. Summers are warm but short, and spring frost, summer hail and harvest rain are serious risks. It is a tough environment for a thin-skinned, sensitive grape like Pinot Noir, so quality and quantity swing widely from vintage to vintage.

</Section>

<Section id="subregions" title="From north to south: the sub-regions">

| Sub-region | Main grapes | Style | Key names |
|---|---|---|---|
| Chablis | Chardonnay | High acidity, sharp minerality, little or no oak | Chablis, Chablis Grand Cru |
| Côte de Nuits | Pinot Noir | Structured reds with deep aromas | Gevrey-Chambertin, Chambolle-Musigny, Vosne-Romanée, Nuits-Saint-Georges |
| Côte de Beaune | Chardonnay, Pinot Noir | World-class whites, softer reds | Meursault, Puligny-Montrachet, Chassagne-Montrachet / Pommard, Volnay |
| Côte Chalonnaise | Pinot Noir, Chardonnay | Lighter than the Côte d'Or and fairly priced | Mercurey, Givry, Rully, Montagny |
| Mâconnais | Chardonnay | Round, fruity whites from the warmer south | Mâcon-Villages, Pouilly-Fuissé, Saint-Véran |
| Beaujolais | Gamay | Light, fruity reds | Beaujolais, the ten Beaujolais crus |

**Chablis** lies apart, north-west of the rest of Burgundy, and is cool. It is famous for **Kimmeridgian** soil, a limestone studded with tiny fossilised oysters. Most Chablis is made without oak flavour, so green apple, lemon and wet stone come through vividly. Travel south to the warmer Mâconnais and the same Chardonnay becomes rounder, with ripe apple and peach. You can watch climate change a grape's expression within a single region.

The **Côte d'Or**, the "golden slope", is a long, narrow escarpment running south from Dijon. Its northern half is the Côte de Nuits and its southern half the Côte de Beaune. The grands crus cluster at **mid-slope**, mostly facing east or south-east: spots that catch the morning sun and drain well, yet do not have too thin a soil. The Côte de Beaune is home to the red grand cru **Corton** and the white grands crus **Corton-Charlemagne** and **Montrachet**.

</Section>

<Section id="hierarchy" title="Four levels and reading the label">

Burgundy's classification ranks **vineyards**, not producers. The higher you go, the narrower the name, the smaller the volume and the higher the price.

| Level | On the label | Where the grapes come from |
|---|---|---|
| Regional | Bourgogne (+ grape) | Anywhere in Burgundy |
| Village | The village name (e.g. Vosne-Romanée) | Within that village |
| Premier Cru | Village name + Premier Cru (+ vineyard name) | A designated superior vineyard in the village |
| Grand Cru | Vineyard name only (e.g. Romanée-Conti, Montrachet) | A single vineyard recognised as the very best |

Curiously, many villages added the name of their most famous grand cru to their own. The village of Gevrey added the Chambertin vineyard to become **Gevrey-Chambertin**, and Puligny and Chassagne added Montrachet. So "Gevrey-Chambertin" is village level and "Chambertin" is grand cru. The shorter the name, the higher the rank: confusing at first, fun once you know it. Revisiting chapter 13, "Reading a label", helps here.

</Section>

<Section id="producers" title="Domaines and négociants">

After the French Revolution, Church lands were broken up and sold, and inheritance laws requiring equal shares for all children followed, so Burgundy's vineyards were divided into very small pieces. The famous grand cru Clos de Vougeot alone has dozens of owners. It is common for someone to own just a few rows in one vineyard.

| Type | Meaning | Features |
|---|---|---|
| Domaine | Makes wine from grapes grown in its own vineyards | Small volumes, clear vineyard character |
| Négociant | Buys grapes or wine to make, age and sell | Handles many vineyards for volume and consistency. Large négociants own vineyards too |
| Cooperative | Small growers make wine together | Especially important in Chablis and the Mâconnais |

So in Burgundy, **the same vineyard and the same vintage can taste very different depending on the producer.** That is why people say to check the producer's name right after the vineyard's. When one producer owns an entire vineyard, it may be labelled a **monopole**.

</Section>

<Section id="beaujolais" title="Beaujolais: Gamay and carbonic maceration">

Administratively Beaujolais is grouped with Burgundy, but its character is quite different. The grape is **Gamay**: good acidity, light tannin and plenty of strawberry, cherry and raspberry. The clay and limestone of the south give light, lively Beaujolais, while the **granite** hills of the north give deeper, more structured wines.

The key to the Beaujolais style is **carbonic maceration (macération carbonique)**. Let's see how it differs from the ordinary red winemaking in chapter 11, "Winemaking basics".

1. The grapes go into a sealed vat **whole and uncrushed**.
2. The vat is filled with **carbon dioxide**, and without oxygen, an **enzymatic fermentation begins inside each berry**.
3. Once a little alcohol forms, colour leaches from the skins; when the berries start to burst, they are pressed and fermentation finishes with ordinary yeast.

The result is a wine with **bright colour and low tannin**, and distinctive fruity aromas like cherry candy, banana and bubblegum. In practice, the grapes at the bottom are crushed by the weight above and ferment normally at the same time, so **semi-carbonic** maceration is common. **Beaujolais Nouveau**, the new wine released every year on the third Thursday of November, is the best-known example.

But not all Beaujolais is light. The **ten Beaujolais crus** on the northern granite hills put only the village name on the label, and some make wines that can age for several years.

| Cru | Character (general tendency) |
|---|---|
| Moulin-à-Vent, Morgon | Deeper and structured; with age they come to resemble Pinot Noir |
| Fleurie, Saint-Amour, Chiroubles | Floral aromas and a soft texture |
| Brouilly, Côte de Brouilly, Juliénas, Chénas, Régnié | A range of styles in between |

<Section id="recap" title="Key points">

- Burgundy is home to single-variety **Pinot Noir** (red) and **Chardonnay** (white), and the vineyard-level **climats** set wines apart.
- The climate is **continental**. Frost, hail and harvest rain make for big vintage variation.
- From the north: **Chablis** (cool, sharp Chardonnay), **Côte de Nuits** (reds), **Côte de Beaune** (mostly whites), **Côte Chalonnaise**, **Mâconnais**, **Beaujolais**.
- The levels are **regional → village → premier cru → grand cru**. Grands crus show only the vineyard name.
- Fragmented vineyards mean **domaines** and **négociants** coexist, and the producer's name matters greatly.
- Beaujolais is the land of **Gamay** and **carbonic maceration**. The **ten crus** on the northern granite are deeper and age better.
