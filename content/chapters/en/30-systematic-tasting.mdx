<Section id="appearance" title="Step 1, appearance: clues gathered by eye">

In Chapter 5, "Tasting Basics", we learned the order: look, smell, taste, conclude. This chapter attaches a **fixed list of questions** to that order. The goal of systematic tasting is not to guess the right answer but to **check the same items in the same order, whoever is tasting**. With the questions fixed, like a doctor's intake form, nothing gets missed and your notes can be compared with anyone else's.

> A tasting note is not a review; it is a record of observations. Write down the evidence before the feelings.

Tilt the glass about 45 degrees over white paper or a white tablecloth and look at three things.

| Item | How to look | What to write |
|---|---|---|
| Clarity | Is it hazy, or is anything floating? | Clear / hazy |
| Intensity | Can you see the bottom through the centre of the glass? | Pale / medium / deep |
| Colour | The colour at the centre and at the edge (rim) | One of the colour names below |

You don't need many colour names. Five or six per wine colour is plenty.

Colour is a clue to age. **Whites get deeper with age** (lemon → gold → amber), while **reds get paler** (purple → ruby → garnet → tawny). If a tilted red shows a wide orange or brick-coloured rim, it is likely to be a mature wine. Depth of colour is a clue to the variety: Pinot Noir and Nebbiolo are pale, while Malbec and Syrah are deep.

The **legs** that run down the glass after swirling only hint at higher alcohol or sugar; they have nothing to do with quality. For a sparkling wine, note the size and persistence of the bubbles.

</Section>

<Section id="nose" title="Step 2, nose: condition, intensity, development, aroma clusters">

Smell once before swirling and once after. Ask four questions in turn.

**1. Condition.** Is it clean, or is there a fault? A smell of wet newspaper or a musty cellar can mean cork taint (TCA); a smell like a browned apple can mean unintended oxidation (Chapter 7). If there's a fault, stop there and note it.

**2. Intensity.** If the aroma rises before your nose even reaches the glass, it's pronounced; if you have to put your nose deep in the glass to catch it, it's light.

**3. Development.** How mature is the wine right now?

| Stage | Aromas at the centre |
|---|---|
| Youthful | Only primary and secondary aromas: fresh fruit, flowers, oak |
| Developing | Tertiary notes begin to peek through the fresh fruit |
| Fully developed | Tertiary aromas like dried fruit, mushroom and leather take centre stage |
| Past its best | The fruit has faded and it feels tired and flat |

**4. Aroma clusters.** Sort aromas into three drawers by **where they came from**. When you meet an unfamiliar aroma, start by asking which drawer it belongs in.

| Cluster | Where it comes from | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Primary | The grape itself and alcoholic fermentation | Flowers (rose, violet), green fruit (apple, pear), citrus, stone fruit (peach, apricot), tropical fruit, red fruit, black fruit, herbs (grass, mint), pepper, mineral notes like wet stone |
| Secondary | Winemaking | Yeast and lees (bread, biscuit), malolactic fermentation (butter, cream), oak (vanilla, toast, clove, coconut, smoke) |
| Tertiary | Ageing | Oxidation (nuts, caramel, coffee), bottle age (dried fruit, mushroom, leather, earth, honey, petrol) |

The character of the fruit is a clue as well. Tart, green fruit points to a cool climate; ripe or jammy fruit points to a warm one (Chapter 9).

</Section>

<Section id="palate" title="Step 3, palate: structure and finish">

Roll a sip around your whole mouth, then swallow or spit. To the five structural elements from Chapter 1, add **flavour intensity** and **finish**, and check seven items. For most, five boxes are enough: low, medium-minus, medium, medium-plus and high.

| Item | Where and how you feel it | How to write it |
|---|---|---|
| Sweetness | Sweetness on the tip of the tongue, a sticky texture | Dry / off-dry / medium / sweet / luscious |
| Acidity | How much your mouth waters; check the sides of the tongue after swallowing | Low to high |
| Tannin | How much your gums and tongue dry out, plus texture (soft, coarse, fine-grained) | Low to high |
| Alcohol | Warmth in the throat and chest after swallowing | Low / medium / high |
| Body | Weight in the mouth: skim milk, whole milk, cream | Light to full |
| Flavour intensity | How clear the flavours are in the mouth | Light to pronounced |
| Finish | How long the pleasant flavours last after swallowing | Short to long |

A few tips:

- **Don't confuse sweetness with fruitiness.** Ripe fruit aromas only *feel* sweet; they don't mean there's sugar. Tasting with your nose pinched makes the difference easy to tell.
- **Judge acidity and tannin separately.** Acid makes your mouth water; tannin dries it out. Both can feel "rough", so first ask whether your mouth is watering or drying.
- **Measure the finish by how long pleasant flavours linger**, not bitterness or the heat of alcohol. A few seconds is short; more than ten seconds counts as long.
- On the palate, check whether the aromas from the nose carry through, or whether new ones appear.

</Section>

<Section id="conclusions" title="Step 4, conclusions: quality and when to drink">

Finally, tie the clues together to judge two things: **how good the wine is**, and **when it's best to drink**.

Quality comes down to four questions. Remember them as **BLIC**.

| Question | In a good wine |
|---|---|
| **Balance** | No single element (fruit, acid, tannin, alcohol, sugar) sticks out |
| **Length** | Pleasant flavours linger long after swallowing |
| **Intensity** | Aromas and flavours are clear, not dull |
| **Complexity** | Not one aroma, but several clusters layered together |

Depending on how many of the four you can answer "yes" to, you can record quality on a five-step scale: **poor** (out of balance or dull) → **acceptable** (no faults but simple) → **good** (two or three of the four) → **very good** (most of the four) → **outstanding** (excellent on all four). If there's a fault, note it separately before judging quality. Always attach your evidence to the verdict, for example: "Very good. Well balanced with a long finish, with primary and tertiary aromas layered together."

For when to drink, choose one of four boxes.

| Verdict | When |
|---|---|
| Too young | Tannins are coarse and the fruit is closed, but intensity and acidity are ample |
| Can drink now, but has potential for ageing | Enjoyable now, with enough acidity, tannin and intensity to develop further |
| Drink now; not suitable for further ageing | Its appeal is freshness, or it is already at its peak |
| Too old | The fruit has faded, leaving only acid or alcohol |

Ageing potential is generally greatest when **acidity, tannin (or sugar) and intensity** are all high together. That's why high-acid Riesling and tannic Nebbiolo last so long. Remember, too, that most wines are made to be drunk fresh.

</Section>

<Section id="worked-example" title="Example: one glass from start to finish">

Let's follow a hypothetical glass to see how the framework works in practice. This example note imagines a **Rioja Reserva** a few years after release.

| Step | Observation |
|---|---|
| Appearance | Clear. Medium intensity. Ruby, with a hint of garnet at the rim |
| Nose | Clean. Medium intensity. Developing. Primary: red cherry, strawberry jam. Secondary: vanilla, coconut, a little clove. Tertiary: leather, dried fig, tobacco leaf |
| Palate | Dry. Medium-plus acidity. Medium tannin, soft and fine-grained. Medium-plus alcohol. Medium-plus body. Medium-plus flavour intensity. Long finish |
| Conclusions | Very good. Can drink now, but has potential for ageing for a few more years |

Written out in sentences, the table becomes a note like this.

> Clear, medium ruby with a garnet rim. The clean, medium-intensity nose is developing, showing oak notes of vanilla and coconut and aged notes of leather and dried fig among red cherry and strawberry jam. Dry, with medium-plus acidity and medium, soft tannins. Alcohol and body are medium-plus, flavour intensity is medium-plus, and the finish is long. Acid, fruit and oak are evenly integrated and three aroma clusters are layered, making this a very good wine. Good to drink now, but with enough acidity and concentration to develop for a few more years.

Once the note is done, you can try linking the clues to a region and variety. Coconut and vanilla point to Rioja's tradition of long ageing in American oak; red fruit and medium tannin point to Tempranillo; and the garnet rim and tertiary notes point to the long-aged Reserva category (Chapter 23). But naming the wine in a blind tasting is a bonus. **Accurate observation comes first**, and the conclusion must follow from it.

</Section>

<Section id="recap" title="Key points">

- Systematic tasting is a record of observations that checks **appearance → nose → palate → conclusions** with fixed questions.
- **Appearance**: clarity, intensity, colour. Whites deepen with age; reds fade.
- **Nose**: condition, intensity, development, and the aroma clusters: **primary (grape and fermentation), secondary (winemaking), tertiary (ageing)**.
- **Palate**: sweetness, acidity, tannin, alcohol, body, flavour intensity, finish. Don't confuse sweetness with fruitiness, or acidity with tannin.
- **Conclusions**: judge quality by **balance, length, intensity and complexity**, and readiness by acidity, tannin and intensity. Always attach evidence to your verdict.
- Every flavour in the glass has a reason, and tasting is tracing those reasons backwards.
