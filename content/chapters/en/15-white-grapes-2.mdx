<Section id="aromatic-family" title="Five grapes that speak through aroma">

In chapter 14, "International white grapes I", we called Chardonnay a blank canvas and Sauvignon Blanc a highlighter. Most of the five grapes in this chapter are on the highlighter side. Their own aromas are so distinct that winemakers usually choose to **protect those aromas** rather than add anything. So new oak is rare.

Instead, two keys separate these grapes.

- **Acidity**: Riesling and Chenin Blanc are high in acid; Gewürztraminer and Viognier are low. Pinot Gris sits in the middle, depending on style.
- **Sweetness**: the same grape can make anything from dry to very sweet wine. Knowing how to find sweetness clues on the label becomes important.

> Acidity is a wine's backbone. With a strong backbone, Riesling and Chenin Blanc carry sweetness and still stand tall, lasting for decades.

</Section>

<Section id="riesling" title="Riesling: the king of acidity">

**Riesling** comes from Germany's Rhine valley, and many experts call it the greatest white grape. There are three reasons: **very high acidity**, a delicacy that reflects differences between vineyards transparently, and the ability to age for decades.

| Climate | Aromas | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Cool | Green apple, lemon, white flowers, wet stone | Mosel |
| Moderate | Peach, apricot, lime | Alsace, Pfalz, Wachau |
| Warm days, cool nights | Lime, lime zest, toast | Clare Valley and Eden Valley, Australia |

**Structure**: very high acidity, light to medium body, and alcohol ranging from the Mosel's low levels to the high levels of dry wines from Alsace and Austria. With bottle age it develops **honey, toast** and a distinctive **petrol** aroma.

**Winemaking**: almost always made without oak flavour. Instead, the biggest choice is **how much sweetness to leave**. Everything is possible: dry Riesling, slightly sweet off-dry, late harvest, **noble rot** wines and **Eiswein** from frozen grapes. High acidity carries the sweetness, so even sweet Riesling feels fresh rather than sticky.

| Region | Style |
|---|---|
| Mosel (Germany) | Steep slate slopes. Light, low in alcohol, often delicately sweet |
| Rheingau, Pfalz (Germany) | Fuller, with a bigger share of dry (trocken) wines |
| Alsace (France) | Mostly dry. Power close to full-bodied |
| Wachau (Austria) | Dry, with concentrated fruit and minerality |
| Clare and Eden Valleys (Australia) | Dry, with vivid lime |

**Kabinett**, **Spätlese** and **Auslese** on German labels refer to how ripe the grapes were at harvest. Broadly, the further down the list, the richer and sweeter the wine, but if it says trocken, it is dry. More in chapter 13, "Reading a label", and chapter 24, "Germany, Austria and Eastern Europe".

**Food**: off-dry Riesling is especially good with mildly spicy food. A touch of sweetness soothes the heat and the acid cuts the fat. Try it with tteokbokki, Korean sweet-and-spicy fried chicken or Thai food. Dry Riesling suits grilled fish and pork dishes.

</Section>

<Section id="pinot-gris" title="Pinot Gris / Pinot Grigio: one grape, two faces">

**Pinot Gris** is a skin-colour mutation of Pinot Noir, with pinkish-grey skins. It is a fun example of a grape whose style changes with its name.

| | Pinot Grigio (Italian style) | Pinot Gris (Alsace style) |
|---|---|---|
| Harvest | Early, to keep the acidity | Late, fully ripe |
| Aromas | Lemon, green apple, pear. Subtle | Ripe peach, pear, honey, ginger-like spice |
| Structure | Light body, crisp acidity | Full body, medium acidity, high alcohol |
| Sweetness | Dry | Dry to off-dry and sweet |
| Classic regions | Veneto, Friuli, Trentino-Alto Adige | Alsace, Oregon |

**Winemaking**: mostly without oak flavour. In Alsace, old large casks or lees contact may be used to add texture. Oregon's Willamette Valley made its name with fruity Pinot Gris somewhere between the two styles, and a lot is made in New Zealand too.

**Food**: light Pinot Grigio suits easy-going meals such as seafood pasta, salads and light fish dishes. Rich Alsace Pinot Gris goes with pork, mushroom dishes and smoked salmon, and surprisingly well with sweet-savoury Korean dishes such as braised short ribs.

</Section>

<Section id="chenin-blanc" title="Chenin Blanc: the grape that can be anything">

**Chenin Blanc** comes from Anjou and Touraine in the middle Loire. With its high acidity and readiness to catch **noble rot**, one grape makes almost every style: sparkling, dry, off-dry and botrytised sweet wine.

- **Aromas**: green apple, lemon and pear (cooler years) → quince, peach and pineapple (riper years). Botrytised wines show honey and dried fruit. With age come honey, nuts and a waxy, lanolin-like note.
- **Structure**: high acidity, body from light to full depending on style.
- **Winemaking**: mostly made fresh without oak flavour, but top wines may build texture with old oak or lees ageing. One vineyard is sometimes picked in several passes to make different wines from different levels of ripeness.

| Region | Style |
|---|---|
| Vouvray (Loire) | Dry to sweet, and sparkling. Check the label for sec (dry), demi-sec (off-dry) or moelleux (sweet) |
| Savennières (Loire) | Dry, intense and long-lived |
| Coteaux du Layon, Bonnezeaux, Quarts de Chaume (Loire) | Botrytised sweet wines |
| Saumur, Crémant de Loire | Traditional-method sparkling |
| South Africa (Stellenbosch, Swartland and others) | The most planted white grape. From crisp everyday wines to intense wines from old vines |

**Food**: dry Chenin suits pork, chicken and fish in cream sauce; off-dry Chenin suits mildly spicy food. Try botrytised sweet Chenin with fruit tarts or blue cheese.

</Section>

<Section id="gewurz-viognier" title="Gewürztraminer and Viognier: lavish aromas, low acidity">

The last two grapes are the most flamboyant in aroma. Both are **low in acidity and high in body and alcohol**, so if the picking time is missed, either the acid collapses before the aromas bloom, or waiting for the aromas pushes the alcohol too high. These grapes demand a grower with good timing.

**Gewürztraminer** carries the German word for "spice" (Gewürz) in its name, and it lives up to it with intense aromas. Its skins are pink, and the wine can be a deep gold.

- **Aromas**: **lychee**, **rose**, spices like ginger and cinnamon, tropical fruit. Once smelled, never forgotten.
- **Structure**: low acidity, full body, high alcohol. From dry to off-dry and late-harvest sweet.
- **Regions**: **Alsace** is the most famous, and it is also made in Italy's Alto Adige, Germany, New Zealand and elsewhere.
- **Food**: pairs well with heavily spiced food. Indian curry, Thai food, Chinese food, and pungent cheeses such as Munster.

**Viognier** comes from **Condrieu**, a small region in the northern Rhône.

- **Aromas**: **apricot**, peach, white flowers (honeysuckle). It needs to be fully ripe for the aromas to bloom.
- **Structure**: low to medium acidity, full body, high alcohol. Mostly dry.
- **Winemaking**: many styles keep the aromas with no oak flavour, but top Condrieu may add texture with old oak or lees ageing. In **Côte-Rôtie** in the northern Rhône, a little is co-fermented with Syrah to lift the red wine's aroma and colour. We meet it again in chapter 17, "International red grapes II".
- **Regions**: Condrieu, southern France (Languedoc), California, Australia.
- **Food**: its apricot notes and richness suit chicken in cream sauce, lobster and mildly spicy curry.

<Section id="recap" title="Key points">

- **Riesling** has very high acidity, transparent aromas and great ageing potential (honey, petrol). Made without oak, from dry to Eiswein.
- **Pinot Gris** is a mutation of Pinot Noir. Italian-style **Pinot Grigio** is light and crisp; **Alsace** Pinot Gris is rich and spicy.
- **Chenin Blanc**, thanks to high acidity and noble rot, makes everything from sparkling to sweet. Its home is the **Loire**; the biggest grower is **South Africa**.
- **Gewürztraminer** means lychee and rose, **Viognier** apricot and flowers. Both are **low in acidity and high in body and alcohol**.
- Aromatic grapes are mostly made **avoiding new oak**, to protect the grape's own aromas.
