<Section id="why-steps" title="Drinking versus tasting">

Most of the time, you can simply drink your wine. But if you want to remember wines, compare them or describe them to others, **tasting in a fixed order** helps enormously. Think of coffee cupping or tea tasting. Check the same things in the same order every time, and the differences between wines start to stand out.

Tasting has five steps.

**Preparing** is simple. Pour no more than a third of a clean, odour-free glass, and taste somewhere bright, away from perfume or strong cooking smells. Hold the glass by the **stem**, not the bowl, so your hand does not warm the wine. Temperature and glass choice are covered in detail in chapter 7, "Storage, service, glasses and temperature".

</Section>

<Section id="look" title="1. Look: what colour tells you">

Tilt the glass slightly over white paper or a tablecloth and check three things.

- **Clarity**: is it clear or hazy? Haze can be a fault, or a sign the wine was deliberately left unfiltered.
- **Intensity**: is the colour pale or deep? Seeing whether text shows through the glass makes this easy.
- **Colour**: whites are lemon, gold or amber; rosés pink, salmon or orange; reds purple, ruby, garnet or tawny.

Colour is a clue to grape and age. **Whites deepen with age, while reds fade and turn brownish.** The colour at the rim of the glass shows the change first. Thin-skinned grapes such as Pinot Noir are pale even when young, and thick-skinned ones such as Cabernet Sauvignon are deep.

The streaks running down the inside of the glass (the wine's **legs**) mostly reflect alcohol and sugar, not quality, so don't read too much into them.

</Section>

<Section id="smell" title="2. Smell: three layers of aroma">

Smell once without swirling, then **swirl** the glass and smell again. As the wine spreads over the glass and meets the air, far more aroma rises. Decide the **intensity** of your first impression (light, medium, pronounced), then look for what the aromas are.

Wine aromas fall into three layers depending on where they come from.

| Group | Where it comes from | Typical aromas |
|---|---|---|
| Primary | The grape itself and fermentation | Lemon, apple, peach, strawberry, blackcurrant, flowers, grass, pepper |
| Secondary | Winemaking | Vanilla, toast and cedar from oak, butter from malolactic fermentation, bread and biscuit from lees |
| Tertiary | Ageing | Dried fruit, honey, nuts, mushroom, leather, earth |

Start by sorting broadly: is it fruit, flowers or wood? If it is apple, is it green or ripe apple? If it is a red, is the fruit red (strawberry, cherry) or black (blackberry, plum)? Making a habit of smelling fruit when you go shopping is better training than you might think.

> Naming an aroma is not about getting the right answer, it is about recalling a memory. The more smells you know, the more you can find in a wine.

</Section>

<Section id="taste" title="3. Taste: mapping the structure">

Now take a sip and roll it around your whole mouth. Drawing in a little air pushes aroma to the back of your nose and makes the flavour clearer. Your tongue senses only sweet, sour, bitter, salty and umami; the rest of what we call "taste" is really aroma sensed by the nose. That is why food tastes flat when you have a cold.

Here is the structure table from chapter 1, "What is wine?", turned into a working tool.

| Element | Where and how to check | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| Sweetness | Is there sweetness on the tip of the tongue? | Dry / off-dry / medium / sweet |
| Acidity | How much does your mouth water at the sides of the tongue after swallowing? | Low / medium / high |
| Tannin | Do your gums and tongue feel dry and grippy? | Low / medium / high |
| Alcohol | Does the back of your throat feel warm or hot? | Low / medium / high |
| Body | The weight in your mouth (skimmed milk, whole milk, cream) | Light / medium / full |
| Flavour intensity | How strong are the flavours in the mouth? | Light / medium / pronounced |
| Finish | How long does it linger after you swallow or spit? | Short / medium / long |

At first, **acidity and tannin** are hard to tell apart. Acidity makes your mouth **water**, tannin makes it **dry**. Remember they pull in opposite directions. And finally, the trap from chapter 4: **don't confuse fruitiness with sweetness**. Pinch your nose while tasting and only the real sweetness remains.

When comparing several wines, it is fine to **spit** after tasting. Professionals do it all the time.

</Section>

<Section id="conclude" title="4. Conclude: sum it up in a sentence">

With the information gathered, make two judgements at the end.

**Quality** is usually judged on four criteria.

- **Balance**: does any one of fruit, acid, tannin, alcohol or sweetness stick out?
- **Length**: does the finish last?
- **Intensity**: are the aromas and flavours clear and concentrated?
- **Complexity**: are there several layers of aroma, or just one repeated?

The more of these a wine meets, the better it is. Then guess **whether it is ready to drink now or could age further**. With enough acidity, tannin and flavour, it is more likely to improve with time.

The conclusion need not be long. For example:

*"Pale lemon. Pronounced green apple, lemon and grass. Dry, high acidity, light body, medium finish. Well balanced and fresh, ready to drink now."*

If that note alone makes you guess a cool-climate Sauvignon Blanc, you have succeeded. **Systematic tasting**, which refines this process to exam standard, is completed in chapter 30, "Systematic tasting and final assessment".

</Section>

<Section id="recap" title="Key points">

- Tasting has five steps: **prepare → look → smell → taste → conclude**.
- **Look**: clarity, intensity, colour. Whites deepen with age, reds fade and turn brown.
- **Smell**: swirl to lift the aromas. Sort them into **primary (grape), secondary (winemaking) and tertiary (ageing)**.
- **Taste**: sweetness, acidity, tannin, alcohol, body, flavour intensity, finish. Acidity makes the mouth water, tannin dries it. **Fruity is not sweet.**
- **Conclude**: judge quality on balance, length, intensity and complexity, and guess when to drink it.
