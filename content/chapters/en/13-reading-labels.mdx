<Section id="two-philosophies" title="Region first, or grape first?">

Wine labels fall into two broad philosophies.

- The **Old World**, Europe's traditional regions, puts **where it comes from** first. They believe the land's terroir decides the taste, and the grapes to use there are already set by the rules.
- The **New World**, the US, Australia, New Zealand, Chile, South Africa and others, puts **which grape it is** first. The producer's name (the brand) and the grape are printed large, with the region underneath.

So to read European wine, you need to know that **the region's name is a code for the grape**. Memorise just the main ones and wine shops become far friendlier.

| Region named on the label | Country | Main grape |
|---|---|---|
| Chablis | France | Chardonnay |
| Sancerre | France | Sauvignon Blanc (white) |
| Bourgogne Rouge | France | Pinot Noir |
| Chianti | Italy | Mostly Sangiovese |
| Barolo | Italy | Nebbiolo |
| Rioja | Spain | Mostly Tempranillo |

There are exceptions, of course. France's **Alsace**, Germany and Austria are European but have a tradition of naming the grape on the label. Conversely, the New World increasingly emphasises region names too.

> European labels ask "where?", New World labels answer "made from what?". Both are ultimately trying to tell you how it tastes.

</Section>

<Section id="eu-system" title="Europe's quality pyramid">

The European Union has a shared framework for protecting origin. The higher you go, the smaller the area and the stricter the rules.

- **PDO (Protected Designation of Origin)** – wines must come from a defined, small area and follow strict rules on permitted grapes, growing and winemaking methods, maximum yields and more.
- **PGI (Protected Geographical Indication)** – a larger area and more flexible rules. It is easier to use international grapes or try new styles.
- **Wine without a geographical indication** – shows little more than the country. Grape and vintage may be stated.

Each country calls these categories by its own names.

| Country | PDO | PGI | No geographical indication |
|---|---|---|---|
| France | AOC / AOP | IGP (formerly Vin de Pays) | Vin de France |
| Italy | DOCG, DOC | IGT | Vino |
| Spain | Vino de Pago, DOCa, DO and others | Vino de la Tierra (VdlT) | Vino |
| Portugal | DOC | Vinho Regional | Vinho |
| Germany | Prädikatswein, Qualitätswein | Landwein | Deutscher Wein |

Italy's **DOCG** is a top tier one step stricter than DOC, and Spain's **DOCa** has so far been granted to just two regions, **Rioja** and **Priorat**.

But **classification is not a ranking of taste.** In the 1970s some Tuscan producers made outstanding wines from grapes the DOC rules did not allow, such as Cabernet Sauvignon, and sold them under a lower category. These wines became famous as "Super Tuscans". A classification is less a guarantee of quality than **a sign that the region's rules were followed**.

</Section>

<Section id="germany" title="German labels and the Prädikat">

German labels look daunting with their long words, but their structure is logical. Above PDO-level **Qualitätswein** sits **Prädikatswein**, which is split into six levels by **the sugar in the grapes at harvest**.

| Prädikat | Meaning | Grapes and style |
|---|---|---|
| **Kabinett** | Cabinet (where the finest wines were once kept) | The lightest. Dry to slightly sweet |
| **Spätlese** | Late harvest | Riper grapes. Can be dry or sweet |
| **Auslese** | Selected harvest | Very ripe bunches are selected. May include some noble rot |
| **Beerenauslese (BA)** | Selected berries | Botrytised berries picked one by one. Rich and sweet |
| **Trockenbeerenauslese (TBA)** | Selected dried berries | Only berries shrivelled by noble rot are picked. Very sweet and rare |
| **Eiswein** | Ice wine | Picked frozen on the vine and pressed |

One thing to remember: the Prädikat only describes **the grapes' sugar at harvest**, not directly how sweet the finished wine is. So a Spätlese fermented all the way becomes dry. To know the sweetness, look at the words alongside. **Trocken** means dry; **halbtrocken** and **feinherb** mean off-dry.

Separately, the **VDP**, Germany's association of top producers, classifies **vineyards** much as Burgundy does. From the bottom up: **Gutswein**, **Ortswein**, **Erste Lage** and **Grosse Lage**, and a dry wine from a Grosse Lage is called **Grosses Gewächs (GG)**. More detail in chapter 24, "Germany, Austria and Eastern Europe".

</Section>

<Section id="numbers-and-words" title="Vintage, alcohol and the words for sweetness">

The **vintage** is the year the grapes were harvested. In the EU and Australia, at least 85% of the grapes must come from that year to state it, and at least 85% must be a single grape to name it. In the US the threshold for naming a grape is 75%. A blend of several years is **NV (Non-Vintage)**, as is most Champagne and Sherry.

**Alcohol (% vol)** is a clue to a wine's weight and its region's climate. As we saw in chapter 9, "Climate and terroir", around 12% points to the lighter, cooler side and 14% or more to the heavier, warmer side. A low figure around 8%, as in a Mosel Kabinett, suggests an off-dry or sweet wine with residual sugar.

Sparkling wines state their sweetness with set terms. The most confusing point: **Extra Dry is sweeter than Brut**.

| Term | Sugar per litre (g/L) | Taste |
|---|---|---|
| Brut Nature | 0–3 | Driest |
| Extra Brut | 0–6 | Very dry |
| Brut | Under 12 | Dry. The most common |
| Extra Dry | 12–17 | Just slightly sweet |
| Sec | 17–32 | Slightly sweet |
| Demi-Sec | 32–50 | Sweet |
| Doux | Over 50 | Very sweet |

For still wines, sweetness is shown with each country's own words.

| Taste | France | Germany | Italy | Spain |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Dry | sec | trocken | secco | seco |
| Off-dry | demi-sec | halbtrocken, feinherb | abboccato | semiseco |
| Sweet | doux, moelleux | süß, lieblich | dolce | dulce |

Other words that often appear on labels are worth knowing too.

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Grand Cru, Premier Cru | In Burgundy and Champagne, a ranking of vineyards or villages. In Bordeaux, a ranking of châteaux |
| Classico | The historic heart of an Italian region, as in Chianti Classico |
| Riserva, Reserva | Wine aged longer under the rules |
| Château, Domaine | A producer that owns vineyards. Bordeaux mostly uses château, Burgundy domaine |
| Mis en bouteille au château / au domaine | Bottled by the producer |
| Vieilles Vignes | Old vines. Mostly no legal definition |
| Reserve | In the New World, usually a marketing term with no legal meaning |

</Section>

<Section id="worked-example" title="Reading two labels">

Let's put it into practice. Below are two **made-up labels** created for this chapter, one French and one from New Zealand.

- **Label A** – `DOMAINE ○○ · CHABLIS PREMIER CRU · Appellation Chablis Premier Cru Contrôlée · 2021 · 12.5% vol · Mis en bouteille au domaine`
- **Label B** – `○○ ESTATE · Marlborough · SAUVIGNON BLANC · 2023 · 13% vol · Wine of New Zealand`

| What to read | Label A (France) | Label B (New Zealand) |
|---|---|---|
| What is printed largest | Region and rank: Chablis Premier Cru | Grape: Sauvignon Blanc |
| Grape | Not stated. It is Chablis, so **Chardonnay** | Sauvignon Blanc (at least 85%) |
| Region | Chablis, in northern Burgundy | Marlborough, on New Zealand's South Island |
| Quality category | AOC (PDO). A premier cru vineyard within Chablis | New Zealand geographical indication |
| Vintage | Harvested in 2021 | Harvested in 2023 |
| Alcohol | 12.5%. A light, taut cool-climate wine | 13%. Cool, but with strong sunshine |
| Producer information | Bottled by the domaine | The producer's name acts as a brand |
| Expected taste | High acidity, lemon and green apple, a mineral, wet-stone feel | High acidity, passion fruit and grapefruit, fresh grass and green pepper |

Both are crisp cool-climate whites, but the labels present their information in opposite ways. Label A says "know this land and you will know the taste"; Label B says "it's this grape, so it tastes like this".

One more for practice. `Mosel · Riesling Spätlese trocken · Prädikatswein · 2022 · 12.5% vol` is a Riesling from Germany's Mosel, picked late when very ripe and made **dry**. As we saw above, Spätlese tells you the sugar at harvest and trocken tells you the taste of the finished wine. The taste of each grape is covered in chapter 14, "International white grapes I", and chapter 15, "International white grapes II".

<Section id="recap" title="Key points">

- The **Old World** puts the region first, the **New World** the grape. In Europe, **the region's name is a code for the grape**.
- The EU framework is **PDO > PGI > no geographical indication**. France **AOC/AOP, IGP**; Italy **DOCG, DOC, IGT**; Spain **DOCa, DO, VdlT**; Germany **Prädikatswein, Qualitätswein, Landwein**.
- The German Prädikat runs **Kabinett → Spätlese → Auslese → BA → TBA** (+ Eiswein). It refers to sugar at harvest; sweetness is shown separately with words like **trocken**.
- In the EU, vintage and grape labelling follow the **85% rule**. **NV** wines blend several years.
- Sparkling wine gets sweeter in the order **Brut Nature → Extra Brut → Brut → Extra Dry → Sec → Demi-Sec → Doux**.
