<Section id="map" title="A wine map divided by rivers">

Bordeaux is a port city in south-west France near the Atlantic, and the vast wine region surrounding it. Open a map and the first thing you see is three bodies of water: the **Garonne** flowing in from the south-east, the **Dordogne** from the east, and the wide estuary where they meet and flow out to sea, the **Gironde**.

Facing the direction the water flows, the land on your left is the **Left Bank** and the land on your right is the **Right Bank**. The land between the two rivers is **Entre-Deux-Mers**, "between two seas".

The region is a prime example of the **maritime climate** from chapter 9, "Climate and terroir". The Atlantic keeps winters mild and summers from getting too hot, but rainfall is high and the weather varies a lot from year to year. That makes the **vintage** an unusually important piece of information in Bordeaux.

</Section>

<Section id="blending" title="Why blend? The logic of blending">

Red Bordeaux is almost always a blend of several grapes. The stars are the three we met in chapter 16, "International red grapes I".

| Grape | Role | Analogy |
|---|---|---|
| Cabernet Sauvignon | Backbone. High tannin and acid, black fruit. Ripens late | The ribs in a braise |
| Merlot | Flesh. Soft tannin, plummy fruit. Ripens early | The tender vegetables and sauce |
| Cabernet Franc | Aroma. Red fruit, herbs and flowers | The garnish |
| Petit Verdot, Malbec | Supporting roles adding a little colour and spice | A final few drops of sesame oil |

Before it is an art of flavour, blending is **insurance**. Plant grapes that ripen at different times and, if spring frost or autumn rain ruins one, the others hold up. You simply raise the share of whichever grape ripened best that year.

> A Bordeaux blend is not a recipe but that year's answer sheet. Even at the same château, the proportions change every year.

White Bordeaux blends **Sauvignon Blanc** (crisp acidity and grassy aromas) with **Sémillon** (round texture, ageing potential), sometimes adding a little fragrant **Muscadelle**.

</Section>

<Section id="banks" title="Left Bank, Right Bank and the sub-regions">

Within Bordeaux, different soils mean different lead grapes. As we saw in chapter 9, **gravel** drains well and stores the day's heat, helping late-ripening Cabernet Sauvignon, while **clay** is cool and holds water, suiting early-ripening Merlot.

| Sub-region | Location · soil | Lead grapes | Style |
|---|---|---|---|
| Médoc / Haut-Médoc | Left Bank, west of the Gironde. Gravel | Cabernet Sauvignon | Firmly tannic, long-ageing reds |
| Graves / Pessac-Léognan | Left Bank, south of the city. Gravel | Cabernet Sauvignon, Merlot / Sauvignon Blanc, Sémillon | Famous for both reds and oak-aged dry whites |
| Sauternes / Barsac | Left Bank, south of Graves where the Ciron joins | Sémillon, Sauvignon Blanc | Botrytised sweet whites |
| Saint-Émilion | Right Bank. Limestone plateau, clay | Merlot, Cabernet Franc | Soft, generous reds |
| Pomerol | Right Bank. Clay, gravel | Merlot | Rich reds with a velvety texture |
| Entre-Deux-Mers | Between the rivers. Mixed soils | Sauvignon Blanc, Sémillon | Crisp dry whites (reds sold as Bordeaux AOC) |

Within the Médoc, famous villages (communes) line the estuary. From the north: **Saint-Estèphe** (firm and rustic), **Pauillac** (power and depth, home to three first growths), **Saint-Julien** (balance and elegance) and **Margaux** (fragrant and delicate). A village name on the label means the wine is made only from that village's grapes. As we learned in chapter 13, "Reading a label", the narrower the name, the higher the expectations, broadly speaking.

</Section>

<Section id="sauternes" title="Sauternes: gold made by a mould">

Sauternes and Barsac are the world's most famous **noble rot wine** regions. The secret is a small river, the **Ciron**. Where the cold Ciron, flowing through forest, meets the warmer Garonne, mist rises every autumn morning.

1. **Morning mist** encourages a fungus called **Botrytis cinerea** on the grapes.
2. The fungus pierces the skins with tiny holes.
3. **Afternoon sun and wind** draw water out through the holes, so the grapes shrivel like raisins and their sugar and acid concentrate.

This "good mould" does not develop evenly, so pickers pass through the vineyard several times, picking only bunches or berries at the right stage of rot. Yields are tiny and the work is intensive, so the wine is inevitably expensive. Aromas of honey, apricot and marmalade, with high acidity carrying the sweetness, let it age for decades. How noble rot wines are made is covered again with Tokaji in chapter 29, "Sparkling, sweet and fortified".

</Section>

<Section id="classification" title="Classifications and en primeur">

Bordeaux has not one classification but several. Where Burgundy ranks **vineyards**, Bordeaux ranks the **château**, the estate that makes the wine.

The **Classification of 1855** was drawn up for the Paris Exposition, dividing the red châteaux of the Médoc (plus one in Graves) into five tiers, from first to fifth growth, based on the prices of the day. There are only five first-growth reds.

| First growth | Village |
|---|---|
| Château Lafite Rothschild | Pauillac |
| Château Latour | Pauillac |
| Château Mouton Rothschild (promoted from second growth in 1973) | Pauillac |
| Château Margaux | Margaux |
| Château Haut-Brion | Pessac-Léognan (Graves) |

The sweet wines of Sauternes and Barsac were ranked the same year, with **Château d'Yquem** given a tier above all others, "Premier Cru Supérieur". Apart from Mouton's promotion, the classification has barely changed since.

By contrast, the Right Bank's **Saint-Émilion classification** was created in 1955 and is **revised roughly every ten years**. At the top are Premier Grand Cru Classé A and B, with Grand Cru Classé below. Pomerol has no official classification at all, yet some of the world's most expensive wines come from there. Graves also has its own classification, drawn up in the 1950s.

Famous châteaux sell their wine in a distinctive way called **en primeur**. In the spring after harvest, critics and merchants taste the wine still in barrel, a price is set, buyers pay in advance, and they receive the bottles a year or two later, a kind of futures contract. Many châteaux also make a **second wine** from younger vines or less outstanding barrels, offering a taste of a great château's style at a gentler price.

</Section>

<Section id="south-west" title="The South West: Bordeaux's characterful neighbours">

The **South West (Sud-Ouest)**, stretching east and south of Bordeaux, is less well known but full of characterful wines.

| Region | Main grapes | Style |
|---|---|---|
| Bergerac | Bordeaux grapes (Merlot, Cabernet, Sémillon, Sauvignon Blanc) | Reds and whites resembling Bordeaux. Nearby Monbazillac makes botrytised sweet wine |
| Cahors | Malbec (locally Côt) | Very dark, firmly tannic reds. Long called "black wine" |
| Madiran | Tannat | Very tannic, long-ageing reds |

Cahors made its name with **Malbec** long before Mendoza in Argentina. The same grape gives a firmer, earthier wine in cooler Cahors and a softer, fruitier one in sunny Mendoza, a fine example of chapter 9's lesson that climate changes a grape's face.

Madiran's **Tannat** is so tannic that **micro-oxygenation**, feeding tiny amounts of oxygen into the wine to soften its tannins, was developed here. Remember the relationship between oxygen and tannin from chapter 12, "Oak and ageing", and it is easy to understand.

</Section>

<Section id="recap" title="Key points">

- Bordeaux's **maritime climate** means big vintage variation, which is why **blending** several grapes developed.
- The **Left Bank** (Médoc, Graves) centres on **Cabernet Sauvignon** on gravel; the **Right Bank** (Saint-Émilion, Pomerol) on **Merlot** on clay.
- The great Médoc villages from the north: **Saint-Estèphe, Pauillac, Saint-Julien, Margaux**.
- **Sauternes** makes world-class sweet wine thanks to **noble rot** nurtured by mists from the Ciron.
- The **1855** first-growth reds are Lafite Rothschild, Latour, Mouton Rothschild, Margaux and Haut-Brion. The **Saint-Émilion classification** is revised regularly, and **Pomerol** has no official classification.
- The South West offers **Malbec from Cahors**, **Tannat from Madiran** and Bordeaux-like **Bergerac**.
