<Section id="four-questions" title="Sorting wine with four questions">

There are thousands of wine names, but ask these four questions of any unfamiliar bottle and you will have a fair idea of what it is.

1. **Does it have bubbles?** If so it is sparkling, if not, still.
2. **Was spirit added?** Then it is a fortified wine.
3. **What colour is it?** White, rosé or red (and the slightly special orange).
4. **How sweet is it?** Anywhere from dry to sweet.

As we saw in chapter 1, "What is wine?", nearly all these differences come from **how fermentation is handled**. Trap the carbon dioxide and you get sparkling wine. Add spirit and you get fortified wine. How long the skins steep decides the colour, and how much sugar is left decides the sweetness.

| Family | Alcohol (approx.) | What it is | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| Still wine | 9–15% | Ordinary wine without bubbles. Most wine belongs here | Red Bordeaux, Chablis, Provence rosé |
| Sparkling wine | Usually 11–12.5% | Dissolved carbon dioxide makes bubbles | Champagne, Cava, Prosecco |
| Fortified wine | 15–22% | Grape spirit added to raise the alcohol | Sherry, Port, Madeira |

> Classifying wine is simpler than it looks. Just ask what happened while the yeast was at work.

</Section>

<Section id="colours" title="By colour: white, rosé, red and orange">

The colour of a still wine is decided by **how long the juice stayed in contact with the skins**. It is a bit like tea: the longer the leaves steep, the darker the brew.

| Colour | How it is made | What it tastes like |
|---|---|---|
| White | Mostly white grapes. Skins removed at once and only the juice fermented | Almost no tannin, crisp acidity |
| Rosé | Black grape skins steeped for a few hours to a day, then removed | Light and fruity. Strawberry, red berries |
| Red | Black grapes fermented with their skins to extract colour and tannin | Has tannin, body ranges widely |
| Orange | White grapes fermented on their skins (the red-wine method) | Amber, a little tannin, dried fruit and tea notes |

One thing to remember: in Europe, **making rosé by blending red and white is mostly banned**. Rosé Champagne is the exception. **Orange wine** may look like a recent trend, but its ancient root is the Georgian qvevri wine we met in chapter 2, "Wine history I".

How each style is actually made is covered step by step in chapter 11, "Winemaking basics".

<Section id="sweetness" title="By sweetness: from dry to sweet">

Sweetness depends on how much sugar is left after fermentation, the **residual sugar**. For everyday use, four levels are enough.

| Level | How it feels | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Dry | Almost no sweetness you can taste | Most reds, Chablis, Sauvignon Blanc |
| Off-dry | A hint of sweetness | Some German Riesling, Vouvray demi-sec |
| Medium sweet | Clearly sweet | Some German Spätlese, White Zinfandel |
| Sweet | As sweet as dessert | Sauternes, Tokaji Aszú, Icewine, Port |

The ways to make a sweet wine also boil down to a few.

- **Stop fermentation early**: chill the wine or filter out the yeast to leave sugar behind.
- **Concentrate the sugar**: pick late (late harvest), dry the grapes (Italian passito), press frozen grapes (**Icewine**), or let **noble rot** draw out the water (Sauternes, **Tokaji**) to make the sugar denser.
- **Stop fermentation with spirit**: raise the alcohol as in Port so the yeast stops.

One caution: **fruity is not the same as sweet.** It is easy to mistake a dry wine full of ripe fruit aromas for a sweet one. You will practise checking whether your tongue actually tastes sugar in chapter 5, "How to taste".

</Section>

<Section id="sparkling" title="Sparkling: where do the bubbles come from?">

The bubbles in most sparkling wine come from a **second fermentation**. Sugar and yeast are added to a finished still wine so it ferments again, and the carbon dioxide produced is trapped. Where it is trapped is the key.

| Method | Where the second fermentation happens | What it tastes like | Classic wines |
|---|---|---|---|
| Traditional method | In each individual bottle | Fine bubbles, bread and biscuit notes from lees ageing | **Champagne**, Cava, Crémant |
| Tank method | In a large sealed tank | Fresh fruit and floral notes, light and lively | Prosecco (**Veneto**) |

Champagne ages for a long time on its lees. Even non-vintage Champagne (NV) must spend at least 15 months, including at least 12 on the lees, before release.

It helps to know the sweetness terms on sparkling labels too. From driest to sweetest: **Brut Nature → Extra Brut → Brut → Extra Dry → Sec → Demi-Sec → Doux**. Brut is the most common. A classic trap: despite the name, **Extra Dry is a little sweeter than Brut**.

</Section>

<Section id="fortified" title="Fortified: when is the spirit added?">

A fortified wine is wine with grape spirit added. **When** the spirit goes in decides how sweet it is.

| | Sherry | Port |
|---|---|---|
| Region | **Jerez**, southern Spain | The **Douro** valley, Portugal |
| Main grapes | The white grape **Palomino** | Black grapes such as Touriga Nacional |
| When spirit is added | **After** fermentation | **During** fermentation |
| Basic sweetness | Dry (sweetness is added later) | Sweet |
| Classic styles | Fino, Amontillado, Oloroso | Ruby, Tawny, Vintage |

Port makes the principle clear. Add spirit during fermentation and, as the alcohol rises, the yeast stops working. The grape sugar not yet turned into alcohol stays behind, giving a wine that is both sweet and strong. Sherry, Port and Madeira are covered in detail in chapter 29, "Sparkling, sweet and fortified".

</Section>

<Section id="recap" title="Key points">

- Wine falls into three families: **still, sparkling and fortified**.
- Colour is decided by **time on the skins**. White, rosé, red, and **orange**, made from white grapes fermented on their skins.
- Sweetness is the amount of **residual sugar**. Ways to keep sugar: stop fermentation, concentrate sugar (late harvest, drying, Icewine, noble rot), or fortify.
- Sparkling wines split by whether the second fermentation happens **in the bottle (traditional method)** or **in a tank (tank method)**. **Brut** is the most common sweetness term.
- For fortified wines, **timing** is everything. **Sherry**, fortified after fermentation, is dry at base. **Port**, fortified during fermentation, is sweet.
