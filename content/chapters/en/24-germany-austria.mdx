<Section id="north-edge" title="At the northern edge of viticulture">

Germany lies quite far north for a wine country. The Mosel sits at around 50 degrees latitude, almost the last line at which grapes can ripen properly. It is a textbook example of the **cool continental climate** from chapter 9, "Climate and terroir".

To ripen grapes somewhere this cool, every scrap of sunshine counts. So almost all of Germany's best vineyards are on **south-facing slopes by rivers**. The river protects against night frost and the slope catches the sun head-on. And the grape that makes the most of these conditions is **Riesling**. It ripens late but resists frost, and keeps high acidity even when fully ripe.

The result: low alcohol, high acidity, and aromas that start with lemon, green apple and white flowers and, with age, move on to honey and a distinctive **petrol** note. The Riesling grape itself was covered in chapter 15, "International white grapes II", so this chapter focuses on "where the Riesling grew".

| Region | Country | Main grapes | Style |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mosel | Germany | Riesling | Light and delicate, low alcohol, dry to sweet |
| Rheingau | Germany | Riesling, Spätburgunder | Somewhat weightier, firmer Riesling, more dry wines |
| Pfalz | Germany | Riesling, the Burgunder family | Warm, generous dry wines |
| Wachau | Austria | Grüner Veltliner, Riesling | Dry, concentrated and mineral |
| Burgenland | Austria | Blaufränkisch, Zweigelt, botrytis grapes | Reds and sweet wines |
| Tokaj | Hungary | Furmint, Hárslevelű | Botrytised sweet Tokaji Aszú, dry Furmint |

</Section>

<Section id="mosel-rheingau-pfalz" title="Mosel, Rheingau and Pfalz">

The **Mosel** is a valley where the river snakes back and forth. The vineyards are so steep that machines cannot get in, and almost all work is done by hand. The **slate** covering the slopes soaks up heat by day and releases it at night, and drains well even when it rains. Sunlight reflected off the river also rises up the slope to warm the grapes.

Mosel Riesling is as light as a feather. Many are off-dry or sweet, but high acidity carries the sweetness so they never feel sticky. Wines of around 8% alcohol are common. As chapter 6, "Food and wine basics", explained, slightly sweet Riesling goes well with spicy food. It is a surprisingly good partner for tteokbokki or Korean sweet-spicy fried chicken.

> Mosel Riesling is like a chilled glass of sweet-tart fruit tea. Sweetness and sourness hold each other in check so neither sticks out.

The **Rheingau** is the stretch where the north-flowing Rhine briefly turns west. As a result, the whole hillside north of the river faces south. Slightly warmer than the Mosel, it gives weightier, firmer Riesling with a bigger share of dry wines. Assmannshausen at its western end is also known for **Spätburgunder**, that is, Pinot Noir.

The **Pfalz** sits just north of Alsace, and mountains block rain clouds, making it one of Germany's warmest, driest regions. It excels in generous **dry Riesling** with ripe fruit, along with Grauburgunder (Pinot Gris), Weissburgunder (Pinot Blanc) and Spätburgunder. It is fun to compare with Alsace across the border, from chapter 21, "France III".

German reds deserve a mention too. As the climate has warmed, Spätburgunder quality has risen sharply, and Germany is now one of the world's leading Pinot Noir growers. **Baden** in the far south, the Pfalz and the Ahr valley are the main areas.

</Section>

<Section id="pradikat" title="The Riesling sweetness spectrum and the Prädikat revisited">

We glanced at Germany's quality levels in chapter 13, "Reading a label". Let's lay them out again. Germany's top category, **Prädikatswein**, is named according to **the sugar in the grapes at harvest**. The key point is that this reflects **how ripe the grapes were**, not how sweet the finished wine is.

| Prädikat | State of the grapes | Common style |
|---|---|---|
| Kabinett | Normally ripe grapes | Light and delicate. Dry or off-dry |
| Spätlese | Riper grapes, "late harvested" | More intense fruit. Dry to medium sweet |
| Auslese | Very ripe bunches selected, some with noble rot | Usually sweet, sometimes dry |
| Beerenauslese (BA) | Botrytised berries picked one by one | Rich and sweet |
| Eiswein | Grapes frozen on the vine | Rich and sweet with high acidity |
| Trockenbeerenauslese (TBA) | Grapes shrivelled like raisins by noble rot | The richest and rarest sweet wine |

So the same Spätlese becomes dry if the yeast eats all the sugar, and sweet if fermentation stops partway. It is the fermentation principle from chapter 1 at work. The clues to sweetness are elsewhere:

- **Trocken**: dry.
- **Halbtrocken**, **feinherb**: off-dry, with a touch of sweetness.
- If there is no statement and the alcohol is low (roughly 8–9% or less), sweetness is probably left in the wine.

The producers' association **VDP** uses its own system that classifies vineyards, as in Burgundy. A dry wine from a top-classified vineyard is called **Grosses Gewächs (GG)**. How botrytised wines and Eiswein are made is covered in detail in chapter 29, "Sparkling, sweet and fortified".

</Section>

<Section id="austria" title="Austria: Grüner Veltliner and the Danube">

Most Austrian wine is **dry**. Austrians speak German and grow Riesling too, but the style is closer to Alsace than to Germany. Where the warm Pannonian plain to the east meets cold air from the Alps to the west, grapes ripen well yet keep their acidity.

The star is **Grüner Veltliner**, Austria's most planted grape, with green apple and citrus and a touch of **white pepper** spice. Light ones are as easy as a cold beer; firm ones are aged. It goes well with vegetables and fried dishes such as schnitzel.

The **Wachau** is a short stretch where the Danube passes through a rocky gorge. Grüner Veltliner and Riesling grow on stone-walled terraces, and the river and cold air descending at night give vivid aromas and high acidity. The Wachau's producers' association uses its own names according to the wine's weight.

| Name | Weight | Features |
|---|---|---|
| Steinfeder | Light | Low-alcohol, refreshing everyday wine |
| Federspiel | Medium | The heart of the Wachau. Balanced dry wines |
| Smaragd | Full-bodied | The ripest grapes. Rich and able to age |

Next door to the Wachau, the **Kremstal** and **Kamptal**, and the broad **Weinviertel**, are also major Grüner Veltliner regions. Austria uses an appellation system called **DAC**, which sets a signature grape and style for each region.

**Burgenland**, on the Hungarian border in the east, is Austria's warmest area. It excels in reds such as **Blaufränkisch**, with cherry, pepper and lively acidity, and soft, fruity **Zweigelt**. And the wide, shallow **Neusiedlersee** (Lake Neusiedl) sends up mist every autumn, helping **noble rot (Botrytis cinerea)** spread. So botrytised sweet wines such as Beerenauslese and TBA are made regularly. **Ausbruch** from the lakeside town of Rust is a renowned botrytised wine too.

</Section>

<Section id="tokaj" title="Hungary's Tokaj: the wine of kings">

**Tokaj** in north-east Hungary is a hilly area where two rivers meet. In autumn, mist rising from the rivers covers the vineyards every morning, and the afternoon sun dries them. These conditions suit **noble rot** perfectly: the berries shrivel and their sugar and acid become concentrated. Hungarians call these grapes **aszú**.

The main grape is very high-acid **Furmint**, with aromatic **Hárslevelű** alongside. High acidity is what keeps an intense sweetness from feeling sticky. Tokaj was also one of the first places in the world to define its regional boundaries and classify its vineyards.

**Tokaji Aszú** is made like this:

1. Aszú berries are picked one by one by hand and collected separately.
2. The crushed aszú berries are steeped in base wine or fermenting must to draw out sugar and aroma.
3. The wine is drained off and aged slowly in oak casks.

In the past, sweetness was shown as 3 to 6 **puttonyos**, the number of tubs of aszú grapes added. Under current rules the minimum sugar for Aszú has been raised, so 3 and 4 puttonyos have disappeared, and stating 5 or 6 puttonyos is optional. Instead of the number, just remember "if it's Tokaji Aszú, it's properly sweet". **Eszencia**, collected only from the juice that runs freely from aszú berries, is an extremely sweet, very low-alcohol rarity, thicker than honey syrup.

Recently, **dry whites** made from the same Furmint are also on the rise. Their high acidity and smoky minerality make them a good match for fish.

</Section>

<Section id="recap" title="Key points">

- Germany is the northern edge of viticulture. The best vineyards are on **south-facing slopes by rivers**, and the star is **Riesling**.
- The **Mosel** gives light, delicate Riesling from steep **slate** slopes; the **Rheingau** firmer Riesling; the **Pfalz** warm, generous dry wines.
- **Prädikat** levels are based on **the grapes' sugar** at harvest. Judge the sweetness of the finished wine from terms like **trocken** and **feinherb** and from the alcohol level.
- **Spätburgunder** (Pinot Noir) is Germany's leading red.
- Austria is mostly dry: **Grüner Veltliner**'s white pepper, the **Wachau**'s Steinfeder, Federspiel and Smaragd, and **Burgenland**'s reds and botrytised wines.
- Hungary's **Tokaji Aszú** is a legendary sweet wine made from botrytised grapes, led by **Furmint**.
