<Section id="map-changes" title="The wine map keeps moving">

Most of the regions we've studied so far built their reputations centuries ago. But the wine map keeps changing in two directions.

The first is **rediscovery**. As we saw in Chapter 2, "History of Wine I", wine began in eastern lands such as today's Georgia and Greece. The native grapes of these countries, long forgotten on the international market, are getting attention again.

The second is **expansion**. As the climate warms and growing techniques improve, serious wine is coming from places like England, where grapes once wouldn't ripen, and from East Asia, with its difficult climate. The principles from Chapter 9, "Climate and Terroir", are being tested on new ground.

<Section id="greece-georgia" title="Greece and Georgia: new faces of old lands">

Greece was the centre of ancient wine culture, but for a long time it was known mainly for **Retsina**, flavoured with pine resin. Over recent decades the quality of its native grapes has risen sharply, and it is in the spotlight again.

The most famous is **Assyrtiko** from **Santorini**, a volcanic island in the Aegean. It almost never rains and the wind is fierce, so vines are coiled low to the ground in a **basket** shape called **kouloura**. The bunches hide inside the basket, sheltered from wind and harsh sun. Phylloxera struggles to live in the volcanic ash and pumice soils, so very old roots survive. Although it grows on a hot island, Assyrtiko has **very high acidity**, with lemon and a salty, sea-breeze mineral character. It is great with grilled seafood. **Vinsanto**, a sweet wine made from dried grapes, is another island tradition.

**Xinomavro** from **Naoussa** in northern Macedonia literally means "sour (xino) black (mavro)". Its colour is lighter than you'd expect, but its acidity and tannin are strong, and with age it develops sun-dried tomato, olive and red fruit notes, earning frequent comparisons with Piedmont's **Nebbiolo**. The soft red **Agiorgitiko** from Nemea in the southern Peloponnese is also worth knowing.

**Georgia** is home to some of the oldest evidence of winemaking ever found. Archaeologists have found traces of wine in pottery about 8,000 years old. Georgia's emblem is the **qvevri**: a large, egg-shaped clay jar buried in the ground, in which grapes are fermented and aged with their skins and sometimes stems. Underground, the temperature stays steady, giving natural temperature control. Qvevri winemaking is on UNESCO's list of Intangible Cultural Heritage. It has something in common with Korea's kimjang tradition, where jars of kimchi are buried in the ground to last the winter.

> A qvevri is wine's kimchi jar. The earth keeps the temperature steady, and time makes the flavour.

Leave white grapes on their skins in a qvevri for weeks or months and you get a white wine with an amber colour and tannin. This is **amber wine**, the style often called **orange wine** elsewhere. It is the red-winemaking method from Chapter 11, "Winemaking Basics", applied to white grapes. The leading white variety is **Rkatsiteli**, the leading red is **Saperavi**, whose flesh is red too, and the largest region is **Kakheti** in the east.

<Section id="england-china" title="England and China: newly opened ground">

**England** was long thought too cool for wine. But as the climate has warmed, vineyards have spread quickly across Kent, Sussex and Hampshire in the south. These hills share the same **chalk** geology as Champagne across the Channel.

English grapes still struggle to ripen fully, so their **acidity is very high**. That makes for sharp still wine but is actually an advantage as a sparkling base. So **traditional-method sparkling wine**, with a second fermentation in bottle using the Champagne varieties **Chardonnay**, **Pinot Noir** and **Pinot Meunier**, has become the core of English wine. With zesty acidity and fine bubbles, it scores well in international competitions. The traditional method is covered in detail in Chapter 29, "Sparkling, Sweet and Fortified". Note that **English Wine** on a label is made from grapes grown in England, while **British Wine** is a completely different drink made from imported grape concentrate, so don't confuse them.

**China** ranks near the top of the world by vineyard area, though much of it is for table grapes and raisins. Its wine regions include **Shandong** on the east coast, **Xinjiang** in the west and mountainous **Yunnan** in the south-west, and the one drawing the most attention is **Ningxia** in the north-centre.

The **eastern foothills of the Helan Mountains** in Ningxia are dry high ground over 1,000 m. Little rain, strong sun and big day-night swings ripen grapes well, but winters are brutal. So in many vineyards the vines are laid down and **covered with soil** in autumn to survive the winter, then uncovered in spring. It is more labour-intensive than anything we covered in Chapter 10, "Vineyard Management". **Cabernet Sauvignon** is the main variety, and grapes such as Marselan are on the rise.

</Section>

<Section id="japan-korea" title="Japan and Korea: the challenge of humid summers">

Wine growing in East Asia shares one challenge: summers bring a **rainy season and high humidity**. Lots of rain while the grapes ripen invites fungal disease and dilutes flavour. That is the exact opposite of most European regions, which are dry in summer.

**Japan**'s signature wine is **Koshu** from **Yamanashi**. It is a pink-skinned variety that has grown in the area for centuries; DNA analysis shows it is mostly vinifera with some genes from wild East Asian grapes. Koshu wine is light and low in alcohol, with subtle yuzu and pear notes and clean acidity, and pairs well with sashimi and delicate Japanese food. Training vines on high overhead trellises (**tana**) to avoid humidity is another feature of Japanese vineyards. For reds, the Japanese-bred hybrid **Muscat Bailey A** is widely used, and in cool Hokkaido, varieties such as Pinot Noir are increasing. In Japan, only wine made in Japan from 100% Japanese-grown grapes may be labelled "Japan Wine" (日本ワイン).

**Korean** wine needs a more careful discussion. Korea's summers are hot and humid and its winters bitterly cold, making vinifera varieties hard to grow. So most Korean vineyards grow **table grapes**, not wine grapes. The most widely planted variety for a long time has been **Campbell Early**, a hybrid with American grape ancestry. It is deep purple, with the sweet, intense aroma Koreans think of as "grape flavour". Muscat Bailey A (MBA), Kyoho and the recently booming Shine Muscat are also grown.

Korean wine made from these grapes is generally fruity, and many styles keep some sweetness. Table grapes often aren't as sugary as wine grapes, so sugar is sometimes added before fermentation. Known areas include **Yeongdong** in North Chungcheong, **Yeongcheon** and **Gimcheon** in North Gyeongsang, and **Daebudo** in Ansan, Gyeonggi, where local farms and small wineries make wine. Korea also has a broad tradition of **fruit wines** made from fruit other than grapes, such as **bokbunja** (black raspberry) wine, apple wine and omija (magnolia berry) wine. Many people also remember **Majuang**, a Korean wine brand launched in the late 1970s.

Korean wine has yet to establish a reputation on the international stage. But producers keep trying to find styles that suit Korea's climate and grapes. Next time you visit a local winery, taste a glass through the lens of climate and variety you learned in this chapter.

</Section>

<Section id="recap" title="Key points">

- The wine map keeps changing through **rediscovery** of old regions and **expansion** into new ground.
- Greece: **Assyrtiko** from Santorini (volcanic soil, basket-shaped vines, high acidity) and **Xinomavro** from Naoussa (the Nebbiolo of Greece).
- Georgia: 8,000 years of wine history, the buried clay jar called **qvevri**, and **amber wine** from white grapes fermented on their skins.
- England: **traditional-method sparkling wine** that makes the most of southern **chalk** soils and high acidity.
- China: Cabernet Sauvignon from the dry high ground of the Helan Mountains' eastern foothills in **Ningxia**, where vines are buried for winter.
- Japan: **Koshu** from Yamanashi, a light, delicate white. Korea: mostly table grapes such as **Campbell Early**, local wine from places like Yeongdong, and a wide range of **fruit wines**.
