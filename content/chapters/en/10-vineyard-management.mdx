<Section id="site" title="Where to plant, and how densely">

Vineyard management starts before a single vine goes in. Once the site is chosen, weighing up the climate, slope and aspect, soil and water from chapter 9, "Climate and terroir", you need to pick the **grape variety** and **rootstock** that suit the land.

Today most of the world's vinifera vines are grafted onto the roots of American grape species, because the root louse **phylloxera**, which we met in chapter 3, "Wine history II: phylloxera and the New World", eats away at vinifera roots. Besides resisting phylloxera, rootstocks help adapt the vine to the soil and control its vigour.

Next comes **planting density**: how closely to plant.

| | High density | Low density |
|---|---|---|
| Examples | Top sites in Burgundy and Bordeaux's Médoc (around 10,000 vines per hectare) | Hot, dry regions short of water (a few thousand vines per hectare or fewer) |
| Why | Vines compete, so each bears less, more concentrated fruit | Each vine gets ample water and nutrients |
| Downside | Labour-intensive and costly | Easy to mechanise, but needs a lot of land |

There is no single right answer. The key is to match the amount of water and nutrients the land offers.

</Section>

<Section id="training" title="Shaping the vine: training and pruning">

A vine is a climber, and left alone it sprawls in every direction. So growers set its basic framework through **training**, and each winter decide how many buds to keep through **pruning**. Each bud grows one shoot, and bunches hang on that shoot, so pruning is really designing the crop.

| Type | Method | Features |
|---|---|---|
| **Head training** | Shoots gather like a head on top of a short trunk | **Bush vines** that grow like shrubs, e.g. old Grenache in the hot, dry southern Rhône and Spain |
| **Cordon training** | Permanent arms (cordons) extend sideways from the trunk | Grows neatly along a wire trellis, easy to mechanise |
| **Spur pruning** | Leaves many short spurs with two or three buds each | Mostly used with cordon or head training |
| **Cane pruning** | Keeps one or two long canes, laid along a wire and renewed every year | The **Guyot** system, common in Burgundy and Bordeaux, is the classic example |

Many vineyards use **VSP (vertical shoot positioning)** trellises, where new shoots are trained upwards between wires. Leaves sit on top and bunches hang together at a set height below, making sunlight and air flow easy to manage.

</Section>

<Section id="canopy-yield" title="Canopy and yield">

The mass of leaves and shoots that grows from spring to summer is the **canopy**. Leaves are the factories that make sugar through photosynthesis, but if they grow too thick the bunches are trapped in shade and ripen poorly, and humidity builds up and brings rot. So growers practise **canopy management**:

- **Shoot thinning** – remove unwanted shoots early and train the rest upwards between the wires.
- **Hedging** – trim shoots that have grown too long to keep the shape tidy.
- **Leaf removal** – strip leaves around the bunches to let in sun and wind. In hot places, though, removing too many can sunburn the grapes.
- **Green harvesting** – cut off some bunches in summer to focus the vine's energy on those that remain.

All of this ultimately ties to **yield**. In Europe it is shown as hectolitres of wine per hectare (**hl/ha**). Fine-wine regions usually sit at a few dozen hl/ha, while bulk-wine regions can top 100 hl/ha. European appellation rules set a maximum yield for each region.

> In general, the less fruit, the more concentrated each berry. But the answer is not simply "less"; it is as much as that vine can ripen well.

Old vines are thought to bear less fruit and root deeper, giving concentrated grapes. That is why labels say **Vieilles Vignes (old vines)**, though most countries have no legal definition of how old a vine must be.

</Section>

<Section id="water-disease" title="Water and disease">

With too much water a vine grows nothing but leaves; with too little, ripening stalls. In dry places such as Chile, Argentina, Australia and California, **irrigation** is often essential. **Drip irrigation**, which delivers just enough water near the roots, is widely used. Many traditional European regions, on the other hand, have restricted irrigation of fine-wine vineyards by law.

The vineyard's greatest enemies are fungal diseases.

| Disease | Favoured conditions | Symptoms | Typical control |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Powdery mildew (oidium)** | Warm, shady spots. Spreads even without rain | White powder on leaves and fruit. Berries split | Sulphur |
| **Downy mildew** | Warm, wet, humid weather | White down under the leaves, leaves and bunches shrivel | Copper (Bordeaux mixture) |
| **Botrytis bunch rot (grey rot)** | Humidity and tightly packed bunches | Bunches covered in grey mould and rotting | Air flow, leaf removal, sprays |

Powdery and downy mildew both crossed from America to Europe in the 19th century. **Botrytis cinerea**, the cause of grey rot, can under special conditions make prized sweet wines instead. This **noble rot** story is told in chapter 29, "Sparkling, sweet and fortified".

</Section>

<Section id="farming" title="Organic, biodynamic, and the harvest">

The philosophy you farm by is an important choice too.

- **Conventional** – uses synthetic fertilisers and pesticides as needed.
- **Sustainable** – reduces chemical use and also considers water, energy and working conditions. Certification standards vary by country and organisation.
- **Organic** – no synthetic fertilisers, herbicides or synthetic pesticides. Traditional treatments such as sulphur and copper are allowed within set limits.
- **Biodynamic** – began with Rudolf Steiner's lectures in the 1920s. On top of organic principles it adds unusual preparations, such as cow horns filled with manure, buried and later used, and a work calendar that follows lunar and astral cycles. Opinions differ on how well it works, but many renowned producers, in Burgundy, the Loire and beyond, follow it.

The last job of the year is the **harvest**.

| | Hand harvesting | Machine harvesting |
|---|---|---|
| How | People cut bunches with secateurs | A machine shakes the vine and knocks off the berries |
| Pros | Damaged bunches can be sorted out, bunches picked whole | Fast and cheap. Can pick on cool nights |
| Cons | Slow, expensive and labour-intensive | No selection, and berries may split |
| Where it is essential | Steep slopes (Mosel, Douro), Champagne with its whole-bunch pressing | Large, flat vineyards |

At the winery, the grapes may pass over a **sorting table** where damaged berries and leaves are picked out. Now the grapes move into the winery, and the story continues in chapter 11, "Winemaking basics".

</Section>

<Section id="recap" title="Key points">

- After choosing the site, decide the **variety, rootstock and planting density**. Because of phylloxera, most vinifera are grafted onto **American rootstocks**.
- Training is either **head** or **cordon**; pruning is either **spur** (short spurs) or **cane** (long canes).
- **Canopy management** controls sunlight, air flow and yield. In Europe yield is shown in **hl/ha**.
- The main fungal diseases are **powdery mildew (sulphur), downy mildew (copper) and grey rot**.
- **Organic** farming uses no synthetic chemicals; **biodynamic** farming adds preparations and a celestial calendar.
- **Hand harvesting** allows selection and whole bunches; **machine harvesting** is fast and cheap.
