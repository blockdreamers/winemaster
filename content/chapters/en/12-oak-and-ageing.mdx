<Section id="why-oak" title="Why oak barrels?">

Once upon a time, wooden barrels were simply the most practical way to store and transport wine. **Oak** in particular was strong, bent well and did not leak, making it ideal for barrels. Then people noticed that wine kept in oak became softer and richer.

An oak barrel gives a wine three things.

| What it gives | How | Effect on the wine |
|---|---|---|
| **Aroma** | Compounds from the wood dissolve into the wine | Vanilla, toast, clove, cedar, coconut, coffee |
| **Tannin** | The wood's own tannin is added | The structure becomes firmer |
| **Slow oxygen contact** | Tiny amounts of oxygen seep through the wood | Red wine tannins soften and colour stabilises |

A **stainless steel tank**, by contrast, adds no aroma and keeps oxygen out. It suits wines whose fruit you want to keep exactly as it is. If an oak barrel is meat grilled over charcoal, a steel tank is meat gently poached to keep its own flavour. Neither is better; you choose according to the taste you want.

</Section>

<Section id="oak-choices" title="Four oak variables">

The single phrase "oak aged" hides many choices. The same wine can turn out very differently depending on the barrel.

| Variable | Options | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| **Origin** | French oak | Tight grain, subtle aromas. Delicate spice, toast |
| | American oak | Strong vanilla, coconut, sweet spice. Traditionally favoured in Rioja |
| **New or used** | New barrels | The strongest oak flavour |
| | Barrels used many times | The flavour weakens with each use; after a few years they become **neutral barrels** that give almost none |
| **Size** | Small barrels (such as the 225 L Bordeaux **barrique**) | A lot of wood contact relative to the volume of wine, so a big effect |
| | Large vats (the **foudre** of several thousand litres, Italy's **botti**) | Little aroma, slow refinement through oxygen contact |
| **Toast** | Light toast | The wood's own aromas remain |
| | Heavy toast | Smoky, coffee and toasted bread notes |

When barrels are made, the staves are heated over a fire to bend them, and how much the inside is charred is called the **toast**.

Oak barrels are expensive. So for cheaper wines, **oak chips** or wooden planks called **oak staves** are sometimes put into stainless steel tanks to add oak flavour at low cost. They add aroma quickly, but cannot replace the slow oxygen exchange of barrel ageing.

> Oak is a seasoning. Used well it lifts the ingredients; used too much, you taste wood instead of wine.

</Section>

<Section id="mlf" title="Malolactic conversion: softening the acid">

After alcoholic fermentation, many wines go through one more change. **Lactic acid bacteria** convert the wine's **malic acid** into **lactic acid**. This is **malolactic conversion (MLF)**. Strictly speaking it is the work of bacteria, not a yeast fermentation, which is why it is often called a "conversion".

- Malic acid is sharp like green apple; lactic acid is soft like milk. So **the acidity softens**.
- **Buttery** aromas can appear. It is one reason rich Chardonnay smells of butter and cream.
- The wine becomes more microbiologically **stable**, which prevents a late malolactic conversion in the bottle that would make the wine cloudy.

| Wine | Malolactic conversion |
|---|---|
| Red wine | Almost always |
| Chardonnay (Burgundy and elsewhere) | Often fully or partly |
| Aromatic, crisp whites such as Riesling and Sauvignon Blanc | Often blocked to keep acidity and fruit |

</Section>

<Section id="lees" title="Lees ageing and bâtonnage">

After fermentation, dead yeast settles to the bottom. These are the **lees**. Left with the wine instead of being removed at once, the yeast breaks down and adds a **soft, creamy texture** and flavours of **bread and biscuit**. Think of how unfiltered beer or cloudy rice wine tastes fuller and nuttier when you swirl the sediment back in.

- **Sur lie** – French for "on the lees". Muscadet from the Loire is the classic example. It adds texture and flavour to a light white.
- **Bâtonnage** – stirring the lees in the barrel with a rod to spread them back through the wine. A traditional technique for Burgundy Chardonnay.
- **Autolysis** – long ageing on the lees in the bottle, as in Champagne, creates distinctive aromas of brioche and toast. Even non-vintage Champagne must spend at least 12 months on the lees and at least 15 months in total before release. More in chapter 29, "Sparkling, sweet and fortified".

</Section>

<Section id="bottle" title="Time in the bottle">

Wine keeps changing even after it is bottled. Tiny amounts of oxygen and the wine's own components react slowly, changing aroma and flavour. Wine aromas are usually split into three layers.

| Layer | Where it comes from | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Primary** | The grape itself and alcoholic fermentation | Fruit, flowers, herbs |
| **Secondary** | Winemaking after fermentation | Vanilla from oak, butter from malolactic conversion, bread from lees |
| **Tertiary** | Long ageing | In reds, leather, earth, mushroom, dried fruit. In whites, honey and nuts. In Riesling, a petrol-like note |

With age, reds fade to **garnet and brick**, while whites deepen to **gold and amber**. Tannins soften and fresh fruit turns into dried fruit.

But **most wine is not made for ageing.** The great majority of wines in the supermarket are made to be drunk fresh within a year or two of release. Wines that improve with time are those well stocked with the following.

| What helps ageing | Examples |
|---|---|
| High acidity | Mosel Riesling, Chablis |
| Plenty of tannin | Bordeaux Cabernet Sauvignon blends, Barolo |
| High sugar | Sauternes, Tokaji Aszú |
| High alcohol (fortification) | Vintage Port |
| Concentrated flavour | Common to all of the above |

Some regions set minimum ageing periods before release by law. Red **Rioja** in Spain moves through **Crianza**, **Reserva** and **Gran Reserva**, with longer oak and bottle ageing at each step, and Italy's **Barolo** must age at least 38 months before release, including 18 in wood. These ageing categories return in chapter 13, "Reading a label", and chapter 23, "Spain and Portugal".

</Section>

<Section id="recap" title="Key points">

- Oak gives **aroma (vanilla, toast and more), tannin and slow oxygen contact**. Stainless steel is neutral.
- **French oak** is subtle, **American oak** strong in vanilla and coconut. **New barrels, small barrels and heavy toast** have the biggest effect.
- **Oak chips and staves** add oak flavour cheaply and quickly, but cannot replace a barrel's oxygen exchange.
- **Malolactic conversion** turns malic acid into lactic acid, softening acidity and adding buttery notes. Almost all reds go through it.
- **Lees ageing** and **bâtonnage** add a creamy texture and bready flavours.
- Most wine is made to be drunk young. Wines with plenty of **acid, tannin, sugar and concentration** age the longest.
