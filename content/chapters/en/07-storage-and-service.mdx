<Section id="storage" title="Storing wine at home">

Wine has four enemies: **light, heat, temperature swings and air.** Avoid them and you can look after wine well without any special equipment.

- **Cool and steady**: about 10–15°C is ideal. **Frequent ups and downs** do more harm than the temperature itself. Avoid a balcony in summer, the spot next to the stove, or the top of the fridge.
- **Dark**: ultraviolet light from the sun and fluorescent tubes damages wine. Be especially careful with wine in clear bottles.
- **On its side**: lay bottles with corks on their side so the cork stays moist and does not dry out or shrink. Screwcapped wines can stand upright.
- **Quiet and odour-free**: somewhere with little vibration, away from anything with a strong smell.

Without a wine fridge, the floor of the coolest, darkest wardrobe or a north-facing room is the next best thing. A kimchi refrigerator with a wine mode, common in Korean homes, also works. An ordinary fridge, though, is too cold and dry, so only keep wine there that you will drink within a few weeks.

And remember: **most wine is not made to be kept.** Most supermarket wines taste best within one to three years of release. Only a minority are worth long ageing.

</Section>

<Section id="temperature" title="Serving temperature by style">

Serving temperature changes a wine's taste dramatically. **Too cold**, and the aromas close up while tannin and bitterness stand out. **Too warm**, and the alcohol sticks out and the wine seems flabby.

| Style | Temperature (approx.) | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Sweet wine | Well chilled, 6–8°C | Sauternes, Icewine |
| Sparkling wine | Well chilled, 6–10°C | Champagne, Prosecco, Cava |
| Light- to medium-bodied white | Chilled, 7–10°C | Sauvignon Blanc, Riesling, Pinot Grigio |
| Medium- to full-bodied oaked white | Lightly chilled, 10–13°C | Oaked Chardonnay |
| Light-bodied red | Lightly chilled, about 13°C | Beaujolais, light Pinot Noir |
| Medium- to full-bodied red | A little below room temperature, 15–18°C | Bordeaux, Rioja, Barossa Shiraz |

The common mistake is to serve **whites too cold and reds too warm**. "Room temperature" for reds meant the cool European rooms of the days before central heating. Today's living rooms are usually warmer, so putting a red in the fridge for 15–20 minutes before drinking is a good idea. Conversely, let a white straight out of the fridge sit for a few minutes and its aromas will wake up.

To chill a bottle quickly, use an **ice bucket filled with ice and water**. Water touches the whole bottle, so it cools far faster than ice alone.

> Wine temperature is a matter of "a little colder, or a little less cold". Whites less cold than you think, reds cooler than you think.

</Section>

<Section id="glasses" title="Choosing glasses and how much to pour">

There are many kinds of wine glass, but the principle is simple. A **tulip shape, narrower at the rim than the bowl**, gathers the aromas inside the glass and sends them up to your nose. A generous bowl leaves room to swirl.

<figure>
  <Ch07Glasses labels={{ flute: 'Sparkling (flute)', white: 'White', red: 'Red', fill: 'Dotted line: how far to fill' }} />
  <figcaption>All three glasses are narrower at the rim than the bowl. The red glass is the largest, the sparkling glass the narrowest and tallest.</figcaption>
</figure>

| Glass | Shape | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Red | Large, wide bowl | More surface in contact with air draws out complex aromas |
| White | Medium size | Gathers aroma while keeping the wine cool |
| Sparkling | Tall, narrow flute, or a tulip | Bubbles last longer and you can enjoy them rising in streams |
| Fortified | Small glass | High alcohol, so you sip a little at a time |

If you only have one glass, a medium tulip-shaped white wine glass suits almost every wine. As for **how much to pour**, about a third of the glass for reds and whites and about two thirds for sparkling is right. That leaves room to swirl.

Keep glasses clean. Leftover detergent kills sparkling wine's bubbles, and cupboard smells dull the aromas. Rinse with hot water and polish with a lint-free cloth.

</Section>

<Section id="opening" title="Opening sparkling wine and decanting">

**Opening sparkling wine safely.** The pressure in the bottle is two to three times that of a car tyre, so a flying cork can hurt someone.

1. Chill it well. The colder it is, the lower the pressure and the less it will spurt. Don't shake it.
2. Remove the foil and, **keeping your thumb on the cork**, untwist the wire cage. From now on, don't lift your thumb.
3. Tilt the bottle **about 45 degrees**, pointing away from people.
4. Grip the cork (and wire) firmly in one hand and **slowly turn the bottle** with the other. You turn the bottle, not the cork.
5. As the cork pushes out, hold it back and ease it out slowly. The right sound is a soft sigh, not a pop.

**Decanting** means pouring wine into another vessel (a decanter). There are two reasons to do it.

- **To separate sediment**: old reds and vintage Port form sediment in the bottle. Stand the bottle upright for a day so it settles, then pour slowly with a light shining through the neck, stopping when sediment appears.
- **To expose the wine to air**: young, firmly tannic reds open up and soften on contact with air.

Very old wines are fragile in air and their aromas can fade quickly, so drink them soon after decanting.

**Leftovers.** Re-seal the bottle and put it in the **fridge**, reds included. Still wines are usually best within a few days, and vacuum pumps or inert gas products help them last a little longer. Close sparkling wine with a special stopper and finish it within a day or two. Fortified wines, with their high alcohol, last much longer.

</Section>

<Section id="faults" title="Telling whether a wine has gone bad">

Thanks to hygienic winemaking, faulty wines are far rarer than they used to be, but you can still meet them. Faults usually show up first in the **smell**. Fortunately most are not harmful, just unpleasant.

| Fault | Smell and look | Cause |
|---|---|---|
| Cork taint (TCA) | Wet cardboard, musty cellar. The fruit is dead | The compound TCA, from corks and elsewhere |
| Oxidation | Colour deepens to brown. Bruised apple, nuts, loss of freshness | Too much contact with air |
| Reduction | Rotten eggs, struck match, boiled cabbage | Sulphur compounds formed when oxygen is scarce |
| Heat damage (cooked) | Stewed jam, cooked fruit. The cork pushes out or the wine leaks | High temperatures in transport or storage |
| Brett | Sticking plaster, stable, sweat | A yeast called Brettanomyces |
| Volatile acidity (VA) | Vinegar, nail polish remover | Acetic acid bacteria and others |

A few more notes:

- Mild **reduction** can disappear if you swirl or decant the wine.
- Some people enjoy a very faint **Brett** as the "earthy" note of a mature red, so opinions differ on where it becomes a fault.
- Some things are **not faults**. The clear crystals on the bottom of the bottle or the cork, the so-called **wine diamonds**, are tartaric acid that has solidified in the cold, and sediment in old reds is perfectly natural.

When a sommelier pours you a little wine in a restaurant, they are not asking whether you like it but **asking you to check it is not faulty**. If it has a fault such as cork taint, you may ask for a replacement. Your nose for faults grows with the smelling practice in chapter 5, "How to taste".

</Section>

<Section id="recap" title="Key points">

- The enemies of storage are **light, heat, temperature swings and air**. Keep wine somewhere dark, cool (10–15°C) and steady, with corked bottles **on their side**.
- Serving temperatures: **sweet and sparkling well chilled**, light whites chilled, oaked whites and light reds lightly chilled, **full-bodied reds at 15–18°C**.
- Use a **tulip-shaped glass narrower at the rim**. Pour about a third for reds and whites and about two thirds for sparkling.
- Open sparkling wine **with your thumb on the cork, turning the bottle**, slowly. Decant to **separate sediment and expose the wine to air**.
- Key faults: **TCA (wet cardboard), oxidation (brown, nutty), reduction (rotten eggs, struck match), heat damage (stewed jam), Brett (plaster, stable)**. Wine diamonds are not a fault.
