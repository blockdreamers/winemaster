<Section id="why" title="Why food changes how wine tastes">

Drink orange juice straight after brushing your teeth and it tastes bitter and strange. Whatever is in your mouth first changes how the next thing tastes. Wine and food work the same way. Take a bite, then a sip, and the food pushes the taste of the wine in one direction or another.

The principles in this chapter build on the structure you learned in chapter 5, "How to taste". Food raises or lowers a wine's **sweetness, acidity, tannin (bitterness and grip), alcohol, body and fruit**. So instead of formulas like "red with meat, white with fish", it is far more useful to know **which taste in the food touches which part of the wine**.

First, the big picture. The taste components of food act on wine in two ways.

- **Making wine harsher**: sweetness, umami and chilli heat. The wine tastes more bitter, more sour and hotter with alcohol, and less fruity.
- **Making wine softer**: salt and acidity. The wine tastes less sour and less bitter, and fuller.

> Salt and acidity are wine's friends; sweetness, umami and heat are the fussy guests.

</Section>

<Section id="hard" title="Tastes that make wine harsher: sweetness and umami">

**Sweetness.** With sweet food, a wine tastes more bitter, more sour and hotter with alcohol, while its sweetness, fruit and body fade. That is why a dry wine with sweet cake suddenly tastes sour and thin. It gives us a rule.

- **With dessert, the wine must be at least as sweet as the dessert.** Honey cakes such as Korean yakgwa, honey-filled rice cakes or a fruit tart call for sweet wines such as Sauternes, Tokaji or late-harvest Riesling.

**Umami.** The savoury taste of soy sauce, fermented soybean paste, mushrooms, aged cheese and seafood broth works in almost the same direction as sweetness. It increases bitterness, grip, acidity and alcohol burn, and reduces fruit and body. **Tannic reds** in particular easily turn more bitter and astringent.

Luckily, umami-rich food usually contains salt as well, which cancels out much of the effect. Still, with very umami-rich dishes such as soy-marinated crab or a mushroom hot pot, **a wine with plenty of fruit and soft tannin** is the safe choice.

</Section>

<Section id="friendly" title="Tastes that make wine softer: salt and acidity">

**Salt.** Salt is the kindest ingredient for wine. It softens bitterness and acidity, and the body feels fuller. Even high-acid or firmly tannic wines become rounder with salty food. That is why salted grilled pork belly goes so well with a sturdy red.

**Acidity.** Acidity in food makes the wine's own acid less noticeable, and brings out body, sweetness and fruit instead. But if the wine has less acidity than the food, it turns limp and flat. So the rule is:

- **With sour food, choose a wine with plenty of acidity.** Vinegar-dressed salads, grilled fish with lemon or a tangy salad suit high-acid whites such as Sauvignon Blanc or Riesling.

**Fat.** Fatty food is not in the formal rule table, but you meet it all the time. Here **acidity and bubbles** wash the fat from your mouth and freshen the next bite. That is why sparkling wine suits savoury pancakes and fried food.

| Taste in food | What increases in the wine | What decreases in the wine |
|---|---|---|
| Sweetness | Bitterness, acidity, alcohol burn | Body, sweetness, fruit |
| Umami | Bitterness, acidity, alcohol burn | Body, sweetness, fruit |
| Salt | Body | Bitterness, acidity |
| Acidity | Body, sweetness, fruit | Acidity |
| Bitterness | Bitterness | — |
| Chilli heat | Bitterness, acidity, alcohol burn | Body, sweetness, fruit |

</Section>

<Section id="spicy" title="Chilli heat: the biggest challenge of Korean food">

The wall you hit most often when pairing Korean food with wine is **chilli heat**. Like sweetness and umami, heat makes a wine more bitter and sour and less fruity. And it has one more decisive trait: **heat and alcohol amplify each other.** A high-alcohol wine makes spicy food hotter, and the heat makes the alcohol burn more. Anyone who has drunk a strong spirit with a spicy snack knows that feeling of a mouth on fire.

So spicy food calls for wines like these.

- **Low-alcohol wines**: such as a light Riesling from Germany's **Mosel**.
- **Slightly sweet, off-dry wines**: a gentle sweetness soothes the heat.
- **Fruity, low-tannin wines**: tannin becomes more bitter and rough when it meets chilli.

On the other hand, **high-alcohol, tannic reds** struggle most with spicy food. The rule holds not only for tteokbokki (spicy rice cakes), kimchi stew and spicy braised chicken, but also for Thai, Sichuan and Mexican food.

</Section>

<Section id="korean-table" title="Putting it to work at a Korean table">

Add one more principle and you are ready. **Match the intensity and weight of flavour.** Delicate food needs a delicate wine and rich food a rich one, so that neither overwhelms the other. Below are starting points. They are not right answers; focus on understanding *why* they work.

| Dish | Key tastes | Try this wine | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bulgogi, braised short ribs | Sweet, salty, umami | A fruity red with soft tannin (Grenache, Merlot) | The sweet, savoury marinade makes wine astringent, so it needs ample fruit to hold up |
| Salted grilled pork belly | Fat, salt | A high-acid red (Sangiovese), or sparkling | Salt softens the tannin, acid cuts the fat |
| Tteokbokki, kimchi stew | Heat, sweetness, acidity | Off-dry Riesling, Gewürztraminer | Low alcohol and gentle sweetness soothe the heat |
| Seafood pancake, tempura, fried chicken | Fat, salt | Brut sparkling, high-acid whites | Bubbles and acid clear away the fat |
| Raw fish, oysters | Delicate, umami | Light, crisp whites (Chablis, Sauvignon Blanc) | They don't drown delicate flavours. Tannic reds can bring out a fishy taste |
| Soy-marinated crab | Intense umami, salt | Fruity whites, low-tannin reds | Strong tannin increases bitterness |
| Yakgwa, honey rice cakes | Sweet | A sweet wine sweeter than the dessert | A less sweet wine turns sour and flat |

At a Korean meal, where many side dishes arrive at once, **a wine that gets along with many dishes** is often more useful than one that is perfect with a single dish. Look for good acidity, tannin and alcohol that are not excessive, and plenty of fruit. That is why off-dry Riesling, sparkling wine and light reds are such reliable problem-solvers at the Korean table.

</Section>

<Section id="recap" title="Key points">

- Food changes a wine's structure. **Sweetness, umami and chilli heat** make wine more bitter, sour and hot, and less fruity.
- **Salt and acidity** soften a wine's bitterness and acid and make it feel fuller. With sour food, choose a **wine with plenty of acidity**.
- With dessert, choose a wine **at least as sweet as the dessert**.
- **Heat and alcohol amplify each other.** With spicy food, choose wines low in alcohol, slightly sweet and low in tannin.
- With fatty food, look for **acidity and bubbles**, and always match the **intensity and weight of flavour**.
