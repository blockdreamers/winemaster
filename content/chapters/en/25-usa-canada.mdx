<Section id="overview" title="One continent, four climates">

Wine is made in all 50 US states, but most of it comes from **California**. Washington, Oregon and New York follow, and to the north, Canada's wine is centred on Ontario and British Columbia. Since the 1976 Judgment of Paris we met in Chapter 3, "History of Wine II", North American wine has been discussed alongside the best in the world.

The key to understanding this vast land is the climate framework from Chapter 9, "Climate and Terroir". The coast is cool thanks to a cold ocean, the land beyond the mountains is dry, and the East and Canada have bitterly cold winters.

| Region | Climate | Main varieties | Style |
|---|---|---|---|
| Napa Valley | Warm Mediterranean | Cabernet Sauvignon, Chardonnay | Full-bodied, ripe fruit, oak |
| Sonoma | Mediterranean, cool near the coast | Pinot Noir, Chardonnay, Zinfandel | From cool-site elegance to rich Zinfandel |
| Central Coast | Mediterranean, strong ocean influence | Pinot Noir, Chardonnay, Rhône varieties | Fresh Pinot, dense Syrah |
| Willamette Valley (Oregon) | Cool maritime | Pinot Noir, Pinot Gris | Delicate, red-fruited Pinot |
| Columbia Valley (Washington) | Dry continental | Cabernet Sauvignon, Merlot, Syrah, Riesling | Concentrated fruit, vivid acidity |
| Finger Lakes (New York) | Cool continental | Riesling | Racy dry to off-dry |
| Niagara (Canada) | Cool continental | Vidal, Riesling, Cabernet Franc | Icewine |

</Section>

<Section id="california" title="California: sunshine and fog">

California has a **Mediterranean climate** with almost no rain all summer. There is plenty of sun, so grapes ripen easily. The problem is heat, and the Pacific comes to the rescue. Thick **fog** forms over the **cold California Current** running along the coast, and in the afternoon the hot inland air draws this fog and the sea breeze into the valleys. The closer a site is to the ocean, and the more open its wind path, the cooler it is.

**Napa Valley** is the symbol of California Cabernet Sauvignon. At its southern tip, **Carneros** catches the wind straight off San Pablo Bay and is cool enough for Pinot Noir, Chardonnay and grapes for sparkling wine. Heading north it gets warmer, and the valley floor around Oakville and Rutherford produces rich Cabernet. This is where the blackcurrant and vanilla notes from Chapter 16, "International Red Varieties I", are at their most generous.

**Sonoma** is Napa's western neighbour. It is larger, with far more varied climates.

- **Russian River Valley**: a cool valley where fog reaches deep inland. Pinot Noir and Chardonnay.
- **Sonoma Coast**: a cool zone right on the ocean. Pinot Noir with racy acidity.
- **Dry Creek Valley**: a warm valley with old **Zinfandel** vineyards.
- **Alexander Valley**: warm and inland. Supple Cabernet Sauvignon.

South of San Francisco, the **Central Coast** is a long coastal strip. In Santa Barbara County the mountain ranges unusually run **east to west**, so sea breezes flow deep into the valleys. That makes it cool despite being so far south, which suits Pinot Noir and Chardonnay. Inland **Paso Robles** is hot, and Rhône varieties such as Syrah and Grenache, along with Zinfandel and Cabernet, do well there.

The broad, inland **Central Valley** is hot and flat, and supplies much of the volume wine. At its northern end, **Lodi** is famous for old-vine Zinfandel. For the story of Zinfandel itself, see Chapter 18, "Regional Signature Varieties".

> In California, "it's further south, so it's hotter" doesn't hold. How close a site is to the ocean, and how much fog reaches it, matters more than latitude.

</Section>

<Section id="ava-labels" title="AVAs and reading US labels">

As we saw in Chapter 13, "Reading Labels", US labels usually lead with the **variety name**. The system for naming where the wine comes from is the **AVA (American Viticultural Area)**.

An AVA differs from a French AOC in a crucial way. It **only sets geographic boundaries**, with almost no rules on varieties, yields or winemaking. You can plant Riesling in Napa Valley or grow Italian varieties in Sonoma. Smaller AVAs can also sit inside larger ones: Russian River Valley lies within Sonoma County, which in turn lies within the larger North Coast AVA.

Labels do have minimum standards.

| Label term | Minimum (federal rules) |
|---|---|
| Variety (e.g. Cabernet Sauvignon) | At least 75% of that variety |
| AVA (e.g. Napa Valley) | At least 85% of grapes from that AVA |
| Vintage | With an AVA, at least 95% from that year |
| State (e.g. California) | 100% for California, by state law |

States can set stricter rules. Oregon, for example, requires 90% for major varieties such as Pinot Noir to appear on the label. **Meritage** is an American term for a blend of Bordeaux varieties.

</Section>

<Section id="northwest-east" title="Oregon, Washington and New York">

**Oregon** is centred on the **Willamette Valley**. The Coast Range to the west blocks some of the sea wind, but the climate is still cool and rainy: **maritime**. Conditions resembling Burgundy drew pioneers to plant **Pinot Noir** from the 1960s, and today it is America's byword for the grape. The style weaves red cherry, raspberry and earthy notes together delicately. The red volcanic soils of the Dundee Hills are famous, and for whites, **Pinot Gris** and Chardonnay are strong. Read it alongside Pinot Noir in Chapter 17, "International Red Varieties II".

**Washington** is the opposite. Most of its vineyards lie **east of the Cascade Mountains**, in a **rain shadow** the rain clouds can't cross. Rainfall is so low it is close to desert, so the **irrigation** from Chapter 10, "Vineyard Management", is essential. The water is drawn from the Columbia River and its tributaries. The high latitude gives long summer days, and big swings between day and night temperatures give concentrated fruit with vivid acidity. Within the vast **Columbia Valley** AVA lie Yakima Valley and Walla Walla Valley, and the signature varieties are Cabernet Sauvignon, Merlot, Syrah and Riesling. Cold winters and sandy soils kept phylloxera at bay, so ungrafted vines are common.

**New York** in the East has harsh winters, so vineyards cluster beside large bodies of water. The **Finger Lakes** are deep, finger-shaped lakes carved by glaciers. They store summer heat and soften the cold of autumn and winter. Racy, mineral **Riesling** is the region's pride. Cold-hardy hybrid varieties are still widely grown, and on Long Island, Merlot-led Bordeaux varieties are planted.

</Section>

<Section id="canada" title="Canada: sweetness in ice">

Canada is a very cold place to make wine, so it turned that cold into a weapon: **icewine**.

Ontario's **Niagara Peninsula** lies between Lake Ontario and the Niagara Escarpment. The lake moderates temperatures, and air bouncing off the escarpment keeps circulating through the vineyards, reducing frost. Instead of picking in autumn, growers leave the grapes on the vine and harvest them frozen solid on a midwinter night when the temperature drops to **-8°C or below**. Pressed while frozen, the water stays behind as ice and only a small amount of juice, concentrated in sugar and acid, runs out. That makes the wine very sweet yet high in acidity.

> Icewine is like skimming only the sweet syrup off the top of a half-frozen drink. While the ice holds on to the water, only the concentrated part gets out.

The most common variety is **Vidal**, a hybrid whose thick skin survives the winter well, but icewine is also made from **Riesling** and red **Cabernet Franc**. Canada is one of the world's largest icewine producers. It is an extreme example of the harvest-timing story from Chapter 8, "The Vine's Year", and a sweet wine we will meet again in Chapter 29, "Sparkling, Sweet and Fortified".

In the West, British Columbia's **Okanagan Valley** has vineyards stretching along a long, narrow lake. The cooler north grows Pinot Gris, Pinot Noir and Riesling, while the dry southern end on the US border has hot summers that ripen Merlot, Cabernet and Syrah.

</Section>

<Section id="recap" title="Key points">

- **California** has a Mediterranean climate. Pacific **fog and sea breezes** cool its coastal regions.
- **Napa** means Cabernet Sauvignon. **Sonoma** ranges from Russian River Pinot Noir and Chardonnay to Dry Creek Zinfandel. The **Central Coast** has cool Pinot from east-west valleys.
- An **AVA** only sets geographic boundaries. The baseline is 75% for a variety and 85% for an AVA on the label.
- **Oregon's Willamette Valley** means cool-maritime Pinot Noir. **Washington** grows concentrated reds and Riesling on irrigated, dry rain-shadow land.
- **New York's Finger Lakes** produce Riesling protected by the lakes.
- **Canadian icewine** from **Niagara** is made from grapes frozen on the vine and picked on sub-zero nights. In the West lies the **Okanagan Valley**.
