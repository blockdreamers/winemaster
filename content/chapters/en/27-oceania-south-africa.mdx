<Section id="overview" title="Three faces of the Southern Hemisphere">

Australia, New Zealand and South Africa are all in the Southern Hemisphere, so their harvest falls between February and April. Each vintage arrives half a year earlier than in Europe. All three favour **labels that lead with the variety**, the New World style we saw in Chapter 13, "Reading Labels". Australia and New Zealand were also the countries that led the wide adoption of **screw caps**.

| Region | Country | Main varieties | Style |
|---|---|---|---|
| Barossa Valley | Australia | Shiraz, Grenache | Rich, opulent reds from old vines |
| Eden Valley, Clare Valley | Australia | Riesling | Lime-scented dry Riesling |

<Section id="australia" title="Australia: more than Shiraz">

Australia is huge. Most of its vineyards cluster at the south-eastern and south-western corners of the continent. The hot, broad inland areas produce volume wine, while cool coastal and high-altitude regions make distinctive fine wine. Both the warm and cool climates from Chapter 9, "Climate and Terroir", exist within one country.

**Barossa Valley** is the symbol of Australian **Shiraz**. Shiraz is the same grape as Syrah from France's Rhône. In warm, dry Barossa it becomes a full-bodied red rich with blackberry, plum, chocolate and liquorice. Phylloxera never reached South Australia, so Barossa still has vines more than 100 years old. **GSM** blends of Grenache, Shiraz and Mourvèdre are common too. Compare it with Syrah in Chapter 17, "International Red Varieties II". Neighbouring **McLaren Vale** is also strong in Shiraz and Grenache.

In the hills right next to Barossa, **Eden Valley**, and to the north, **Clare Valley**, sit at higher altitude with cool nights. They are famous for **dry Riesling**: piercing lime and lemon when young, developing toast and honey with age.

To the east, New South Wales's **Hunter Valley** is hot and humid. Frequent rain at harvest means grapes are picked early, and this gave rise to a unique **Semillon**. With alcohol around 10–11% and no oak, it is light and lemony when young, but after several years in bottle it blossoms with **toast, honey and nutty notes**.

> A wine that smells of toast without ever seeing an oak barrel. Hunter Semillon shows the power of bottle ageing from Chapter 12, "Oak and Ageing", more dramatically than anything else.

In the south, **Coonawarra** is famous for a strip of red earth called **terra rossa** lying thinly over limestone. This soil yields firm **Cabernet Sauvignon** with blackcurrant laced with mint and eucalyptus. In Western Australia, **Margaret River** has a maritime climate shaped by the Indian Ocean and is known for elegant Cabernet Sauvignon blends, Chardonnay, and whites blending Semillon with Sauvignon Blanc.

At the coolest end are Victoria's **Yarra Valley** and the island of **Tasmania**. Their Pinot Noir and Chardonnay are delicate, and Tasmania is also highly rated for traditional-method sparkling wine.

</Section>

<Section id="new-zealand" title="New Zealand: coolness and pure fruit">

New Zealand is made up of two long islands running north to south, and no vineyard is far from the sea. The climate is mostly **cool maritime**: the sun is strong but temperatures stay moderate, so fruit flavours are pure and acidity is high.

What put New Zealand on the world map is **Sauvignon Blanc** from **Marlborough**, at the north-eastern tip of the South Island. It appeared in the 1980s and quickly became a global hit. Today most New Zealand wine comes from Marlborough, and most of that is Sauvignon Blanc. Clear, sunny days and cool nights create a big temperature swing, giving intense aromas with razor-sharp acidity.

Once you've smelled Marlborough Sauvignon Blanc, it's hard to forget. Tropical fruit like **passion fruit** and grapefruit, **gooseberry**, and **grassy** notes like fresh-cut grass or green chilli all leap out at once. Most is made unoaked, kept fresh and drunk young. Put it beside the restrained Sancerre style from the Loire in Chapter 14, "International White Varieties I", and you'll feel how different one grape can be. Marlborough also makes Pinot Noir, Chardonnay and sparkling wine.

New Zealand's red is **Pinot Noir**.

- **Central Otago**: inland in the south of the South Island and ringed by mountains, it is one of the few places in New Zealand with a near-**continental climate**. Summers are hot and dry and nights are cold, producing dense, vivid cherry-scented Pinot Noir. It is one of the southernmost wine regions in the world.
- **Martinborough**: a small region in the Wairarapa, at the southern tip of the North Island. It made its name early with structured, delicate Pinot Noir.

On the North Island's east coast, **Hawke's Bay** is one of New Zealand's warmer regions. The gravel soils of the **Gimblett Gravels** produce Merlot and Cabernet Sauvignon blends and Syrah with vivid pepper, and its Chardonnay is excellent too.

</Section>

<Section id="south-africa" title="South Africa: the cape where two oceans meet">

Most of South Africa's wine regions are in the **Western Cape** at the country's south-western tip. It has a hot, dry-summer **Mediterranean climate**, but the cold **Benguela Current** flowing up the west coast and a strong **south-easterly wind** called the "Cape Doctor" cool things down and reduce vineyard disease. Wine has been made here since the 1650s, making it one of the oldest New World regions. **Constantia**, south of Cape Town, was famous in the 18th and 19th centuries for a sweet wine loved by European royalty.

**Stellenbosch** is the heart of South African wine and the home of its wine education and research. Its mountain-ringed valleys and slopes, cooled by ocean winds, are especially good for structured reds such as **Cabernet Sauvignon**, Bordeaux blends, Pinotage and Syrah.

**Chenin Blanc** is South Africa's most widely planted variety. Once called "Steen" and used heavily for bulk wine, old-vine Chenin is now counted among the country's finest whites. Apple, quince and honey with lively acidity are its hallmarks. Compare it with Loire Chenin from Chapter 15, "International White Varieties II".

**Swartland**, north of Stellenbosch, is hot, dry wheat country. Old, dry-farmed **bush vines** of Chenin Blanc and Rhône varieties such as Syrah and Grenache drew in young producers pursuing hands-off winemaking, and Swartland became the new face of South African wine.

South Africa's own grape is **Pinotage**. In 1925 Professor Perold of Stellenbosch University bred it by crossing **Pinot Noir** with **Cinsault**. South Africans called Cinsault "Hermitage" at the time, so the two names were combined into Pinotage. It shows blackberry and plum with smoky and even banana-like notes, and styles range from light to rich and oak-aged. We already met it in Chapter 18, "Regional Signature Varieties".

South Africa's origin system is **WO (Wine of Origin)**, and its traditional-method sparkling wine is called **Cap Classique**.

</Section>

<Section id="recap" title="Key points">

- The Southern Hemisphere harvests from February to April. All three countries use variety-led labels.
- Australia: rich **Shiraz** from **Barossa**, dry **Riesling** from **Eden and Clare**, long-ageing **Semillon** from the **Hunter**, terra rossa **Cabernet** from **Coonawarra**, elegant blends from **Margaret River**, and cool-climate Pinot Noir and sparkling from **Yarra and Tasmania**.
- New Zealand: passion fruit and grassy **Marlborough Sauvignon Blanc**, **Pinot Noir** from **Central Otago** and **Martinborough**, and Bordeaux blends and Syrah from **Hawke's Bay**.
- South Africa: a Mediterranean climate cooled by the **Benguela Current** and south-easterly wind. **Stellenbosch** Cabernet, **Chenin Blanc** (the most planted variety), old vines in **Swartland**, and South Africa's own **Pinotage** (Pinot Noir × Cinsault).
