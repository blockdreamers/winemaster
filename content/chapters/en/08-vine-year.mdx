<Section id="one-crop" title="One harvest a year">

A vine is a **perennial climbing plant** that, once planted, lives for decades. Yet it bears fruit only once a year. Just as a rice farmer stakes everything on a single year's weather from planting to harvest, wine inherits everything that happened in the vineyard that year.

That is why the **vintage**, the harvest year printed on the label, matters. Even from the same vineyard and producer, wines from a year with spring frost, a year with an unusually hot summer and a year with a deluge just before harvest will all differ.

The period in which the vine buds and ripens its fruit is the **growing season**: roughly April to October in the Northern Hemisphere and October to April in the Southern. The rest of the year it drops its leaves and rests, preparing for the next.

> A bottle of wine is a snapshot of a vine's year. That year's spring and summer are inside it.

</Section>

<Section id="cycle" title="The year in seven stages">

The vine's year is usually split into seven stages. In the Southern Hemisphere the seasons are reversed, so everything happens about six months later, or earlier.

| Stage | What happens | What people do |
|---|---|---|
| Dormancy | Leaves fall and the vine rests. Reserves are stored in trunk and roots | **Pruning** sets this year's number of buds |
| Budburst | As temperatures rise, shoots emerge from the buds | Watch for frost |
| Flowering | Tiny flowers bloom and pollinate themselves | Little to do but watch the weather |
| Fruit set | Pollinated flowers become small, hard green berries | Shoot thinning, disease control |
| Véraison | Berries soften and black grapes colour | Bunch thinning, canopy work |
| Ripening | Sugar rises, acid falls, aromas and tannins ripen | Taste and analyse daily to choose the picking date |
| Harvest | The grapes are picked | Pick by hand or machine and take them to the winery |

It is often said that flowering to harvest takes around 100 days. Of course, it varies quite a bit between early- and late-ripening varieties and between warm and cool years.

</Section>

<Section id="spring" title="Spring: shoots, flowers and frost">

When spring comes and average temperatures rise above about 10°C, the buds that slept all winter **burst**. From here begins the grower's biggest worry: **spring frost**.

New shoots are tender and full of water, so if the temperature drops below freezing on a clear, still night, they can freeze overnight. Cold air is heavy and pools in low spots, so vineyards on valley floors are especially at risk. Growers use methods like these.

| Frost protection | How it works |
|---|---|
| Wind machines | Mix warmer air from above down to the vines |
| Heaters, candles | Fires lit around the vineyard warm the air |
| Sprinklers | Water releases heat as it freezes, holding the shoots near 0°C |
| Choosing slopes | Plant on slopes where cold air drains away in the first place |

A few weeks after budburst, **flowering** begins. Vine flowers are small and inconspicuous, but this stage decides the size of the crop. If cold, wet or windy days drag on, pollination fails and the flowers simply drop, which is called **coulure**. Bunches with a mix of large and small berries, known as **millerandage**, have the same cause.

Successfully pollinated flowers turn into small, hard green berries. This is **fruit set**. At this point the grapes are very sour and hard.

</Section>

<Section id="summer" title="Summer: véraison and ripening">

One day in midsummer, the hard green berries soften and the skins of black grapes start to blush red. This change is called **véraison**. White grapes lose their green and turn translucent too. From véraison on, the grapes ripen in earnest.

As they ripen, four things happen inside the berry at once.

- **Sugar builds up.** Sugar made by the leaves through photosynthesis moves into the fruit. Sugar is the raw material of alcohol.
- **Acid falls.** In particular, sharp, green-apple-like **malic acid** drops sharply through heat and respiration. **Tartaric acid**, the grape's other main acid, holds up relatively well.
- **Aromas develop.** Grassy notes fade and the variety's own fruit aromas grow stronger.
- **Tannin and colour ripen.** Harsh tannins in skins and seeds soften and pigment increases. This is called **phenolic ripeness**.

Summer has its dangers too: **hail**, which can shred leaves and fruit in moments; severe **drought**, which exhausts the vine and stalls ripening; and heatwaves that scorch the skins. In hail-prone places such as Burgundy, one storm can wipe out much of a year's crop.

</Section>

<Section id="harvest" title="When to pick: the sugar–acid tug of war">

As grapes ripen, sugar keeps rising and acid keeps falling. So **when you pick** decides **what wine you make**.

<figure>
  <Ch08Ripening labels={{ sugar: 'Sugar', acid: 'Acid', start: 'Véraison', time: 'Time →', windows: ['For sparkling', 'Dry wine', 'Late-harvest sweet'] }} />
  <figcaption>Sugar and acid move in opposite directions. Where you pick along the curve sets the style of the wine.</figcaption>
</figure>

| Picking time | State of the grapes | Suits |
|---|---|---|
| Early | High acid, low sugar. Green, fresh aromas | Sparkling wines such as Champagne, light crisp whites |
| Balanced | Sugar, acid and tannin in balance | Most dry wines |
| Late | High sugar, low acid. Jammy aromas | Rich, high-alcohol wines, late-harvest sweet wines |
| Very late | Frozen or shrivelled on the vine, sugar concentrated | Icewine, some noble rot wines |

Pick too early and the wine is sour and green with harsh tannins. Pick too late and it is sweet and high in alcohol, but the acid is gone and it feels flat and heavy. It is a bit like kimchi: choosing the moment of flavour you want somewhere between freshly made and long fermented.

> Choosing a picking date is not deciding "how sweet" but "what balance".

So in the weeks before harvest, producers walk the vineyard every day, chewing grapes, measuring sugar and acidity and checking whether the seeds have turned brown. Then the weather throws in a variable. If heavy rain is forecast for next week, they may have to pick early even if the grapes are not quite ripe, because rain dilutes the juice, splits the berries and invites rot.

</Section>

<Section id="recap" title="Key points">

- A vine bears fruit **once a year**, and that year's weather becomes the character of the **vintage**.
- The year runs **dormancy → budburst → flowering → fruit set → véraison → ripening → harvest**. The Southern Hemisphere is about six months apart.
- The Northern Hemisphere harvests mostly in **September–October**, the Southern in **February–April**.
- The dangers: **frost** in spring, **coulure** at flowering, **hail and drought** in summer, **rain and rot** at harvest.
- As grapes ripen, **sugar rises and acid falls**. Choosing the picking date means choosing the balance point.
